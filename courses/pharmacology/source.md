## Ch. 1 — Introduction to Pharmacology {#ch01}

### 1.1 Definitions {#ch01-1}

**Pharmacology**: from Greek *pharmakon* ("drug") and *logia* ("the study of") — the
science that deals with effects of drugs on living systems. Includes history, source,
physicochemical properties, dosage forms, methods of administration, absorption,
distribution, mechanism of action, biotransformation, excretion, clinical uses and
adverse effects of drugs.

**Drug**: any substance or product used or intended to be used to modify
physiological systems or pathological states for the benefit of the recipient. A
substance used in the prevention, treatment or diagnosis of disease.

- **Pharmacokinetics (PK)**: study of absorption, distribution, metabolism and
  excretion (ADME) of drugs — "what the body does to a drug."
- **Pharmacodynamics (PD)**: the study of the biological and therapeutic effects of
  drugs — "what the drug does to the body."
- **Pharmacotherapeutics**: proper selection and use of drugs for prevention and
  treatment of disease.
- **Toxicology**: the branch of pharmacology dealing with the "undesirable" effects
  of drugs on biological processes.

**Adverse or "side-effect"**: an unintended action of a drug. Results from a lack of
specificity of drug action. All drugs are capable of producing adverse effects.

### 1.2 Drug names {#ch01-2}

- **Chemical name** — the drug's chemical composition and molecular structure.
- **Generic name** (nonproprietary) — name given by the United States Adopted Name
  Council.
- **Trade name / brand name / product** (proprietary) — registered trademark; use
  restricted by the drug's owner (usually the manufacturer).

Diagram example: chemical name *p-isobutylhydratropic acid* → generic name
*ibuprofen* → trade names Advil, Genpril, Haltran, Ibuprin, Menadol, Midol IB,
Motrin, Motrin IB, Nuprin, Saleto-200, Actiprofen (CAN). To avoid confusion, it's
best to use a drug's generic name.

**Grouping of drugs**:
- Pharmacologic class (family) — drugs that share similar characteristics, e.g.
  beta-adrenergic blockers.
- Therapeutic class — groups drugs by therapeutic use, e.g. antihypertensives.

### 1.3 Principles of prescription writing {#ch01-3}

A **prescription** is a written, verbal, or electronic order from a practitioner or
designated agent to a pharmacist for a particular medication for a specific patient.

**The "Five Rights" of medication administration**: right drug, right dose, right
time, right route, right patient.

Prescription formatting: heading, body, closing.

Dosage-form abbreviations: tab (tablet), cap (capsule), syr (syrup), susp
(suspension), Inj (injection), metered dose inhaler (as such), lotion (as such).

Route abbreviations: PO (by mouth), PR (per rectum), IM (intramuscular), IV
(intravenous), ID (intradermal), IN (intranasal), TP (topical), SL (sublingual),

### 1.4 Routes of drug administration {#ch01-4}

Selection of route depends on: (1) properties of the drug — water/lipid solubility,
ionization, effect of gastric pH, digestive enzymes, first-pass metabolism; (2)
therapeutic objectives — onset/duration, site of action (local/systemic); (3)
condition of the patient (unconscious, vomiting, diarrhea); (4) age; (5)
patient's/doctor's choice.

Major routes:
- **A. Enteral**: oral, sublingual, buccal.
- **B. Parenteral**: IV, IM, SC, ID.
- **C. Others**: transdermal, nasal inhalation, oral inhalation, topical (skin cream,
  eye drops, ear drops), rectal, vaginal, etc.

**1. Oral** — most common and acceptable route.
- Advantages: convenient (self-administered, pain-free, easy), absorption along
  whole GI tract, cheap, safe (low infection risk), overdose/toxicity manageable
  with antidotes (e.g. charcoal).
- Disadvantages: sometimes inefficient (only part absorbed; affected by gastric pH);
  destruction by gastric acid/digestive juices; first-pass effect; gastric
  irritation (nausea, vomiting); too slow for emergencies; unpleasant taste; unusable
  in unconscious patients.
- **First-pass effect**: the intestinal and hepatic degradation or alteration of a
  drug taken by mouth. The greater the first-pass effect, the less the agent reaches
  systemic circulation when given orally. (Diagram: drug → mouth → GI lumen →
  hepatic portal vein/capillary network → liver metabolizes drugs → general/systemic
  circulation.)
- Oral preparation types: **A. Enteric-coated preparations** — chemical envelope
  resisting stomach fluids/enzymes but dissolving in the upper intestine; useful for
  acid-unstable drugs (e.g. omeprazole) and stomach-irritating drugs (e.g. aspirin).
  **B. Extended-release preparations (ER/XR)** — special coating/ingredients
  controlling release rate; slower absorption, prolonged duration, better
  compliance, maintains therapeutic concentration longer; advantageous for drugs
  with short half-lives.

**2. Sublingual/buccal** — placement under the tongue lets a drug diffuse into the
capillary network and enter systemic circulation directly; buccal route places drug
between cheek and gum.
- Advantages: ease of administration, rapid absorption, low infection risk, bypasses
  harsh GI environment, avoids first-pass metabolism.
- Disadvantages: inconvenient, small doses only, unpleasant taste of some drugs.

**II. Parenteral route** — introduces drugs directly across the body's barrier
defenses into systemic circulation.
- Uses: drugs poorly absorbed from GI tract (e.g. heparin), drugs unstable in GI
  tract (e.g. insulin), patients unable to take oral medication (e.g. unconscious),
  circumstances requiring rapid onset.
- Advantages: avoids first-pass metabolism/harsh GI environments, most control over
  actual dose delivered, good bioavailability.
- Disadvantages: irreversible, low convenience (pain, fear), local tissue damage,
  infections.

Diagram (p.32): four parenteral injection depths through skin layers — intravenous
injection (into vein), dermal injection, subcutaneous injection (into subcutaneous
tissue), intramuscular injection (into muscle) — labeled against epidermis, dermis,
subcutaneous tissue, muscle.

1. **Intravenous (IV)** — drugs injected directly into the bloodstream through a
   vein; given as bolus, slow IV injection, or IV infusion.
   - Uses/advantages: for drugs not absorbed orally; for irritating chemicals;
     control over amount delivered; absorption phase bypassed (100%
     bioavailability); precise, accurate, almost immediate onset; large quantities
     can be given, fairly pain-free.
   - Disadvantages: irreversible; may introduce infection at injection site; risk of
     embolism; risk of adverse reactions from too-rapid delivery of high
     concentrations to plasma/tissues.
2. **Intramuscular (IM)** — drugs injected into a large muscle; can be very rapid
   absorption (aqueous solution) or slow-release (depot effect); pain at injection
   site for certain drugs.
3. **Subcutaneous (SC)** — absorption via simple diffusion; slower than IV.
   Advantages: minimizes hemolysis/thrombosis risk vs. IV; may provide constant,
   slow, sustained effects. Should not be used with tissue-irritating drugs.
   Absorption limited by blood flow (affected by circulatory problems).
4. **Intradermal (ID)** — injection into the dermis (the more vascular layer under
   the epidermis). Used for diagnostic determination and desensitization.

**III. Others**
1. **Oral inhalation** — gaseous/volatile agents and aerosols; rapid onset due to
   large surface area and thin alveolar-capillary membranes; effective/convenient
   for respiratory complaints (e.g. asthma).
2. **Nasal inhalation** — drug administered directly into the nose. Local effect:
   nasal decongestants (e.g. oxymetazoline), anti-inflammatory corticosteroids (e.g.
   mometasone furoate). Systemic effect: desmopressin for diabetes insipidus.
3. **Intrathecal/intraventricular** — introduces drugs directly into CSF, when drugs
   cannot cross the blood-brain barrier or local/rapid CNS effects are needed.
4. **Topical** — drug applied to skin or mucous membrane for local effect (eye,
   nasal, vaginal, rectal mucosa; ointment/cream on skin).
5. **Transdermal** — drug administered as a patch delivering drug into systemic
   circulation. Stable blood levels; no first-pass metabolism; drug must be potent
   or the patch becomes too large; variable rate of absorption.
6. **Rectal** — advantages: good for unconscious patients and children, useful if
   nauseous/vomiting, good for drugs affecting the bowel (e.g. laxatives), easy to
   terminate exposure. Disadvantages: variable absorption, irritating drugs
   contraindicated.

> The route of administration is determined by the physical characteristics of the
> drug, the speed at which it is absorbed and/or released, and the need to bypass
> hepatic metabolism and achieve high concentration at particular sites.

## Ch. 2 — Pharmacokinetics {#ch02}

Pharmacokinetics (from *pharmaco*- drug + *kinesis* movement) is "what the body does
to the drug" — absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME).

Why study it: PK properties determine onset, intensity, and duration of drug action,
letting the clinician design/optimize treatment regimens (route, dose/frequency,
duration).

Diagrams: (1) a flow diagram — Drug Administered → [Absorption] → Drug Concentration
in Systemic Circulation ⇄ Drug in Tissues of Distribution / Drug Metabolized
(Distribution / Elimination — the pharmacokinetic side) → Drug Concentration at Site
of Action → Pharmacological Effect → Clinical Response → Toxicity / Efficacy (the
pharmacodynamic side). (2) ADME body diagram: Absorption ("how will it get in?"),
Distribution ("where will it go?", via transporters), Metabolism ("how is it broken
down?", liver), Excretion ("how does it leave?").

### 2.1 Absorption {#ch02-1}

**Absorption**: transfer of a drug from its site of administration to the
bloodstream via one of several mechanisms (except for drugs applied directly to the
target tissue). Rate and extent depend on: the environment where absorbed, the
chemical characteristics of the drug, and route of administration (bioavailability).

- **Rate**: how rapidly the drug gets from administration site to general
  circulation.
- **Extent**: how much of the administered dose enters general circulation (%
  bioavailability = F).

Factors influencing absorption: (1) effect of pH on drug absorption, (2) blood flow
to the absorption site, (3) total surface area available, (4) contact time at the
absorption surface, (5) expression of P-glycoprotein.

Mechanisms of absorption from the GI tract:
1. **Passive diffusion** — movement of a solute through a biological barrier from
   higher to lower concentration (no energy or carrier needed); e.g. highly
   lipid-soluble drugs.
2. **Facilitated diffusion** — passage across the membrane along the concentration
   gradient via a protein carrier (carrier-mediated diffusion).
3. **Active transport** — drugs pass across the membrane most often against their
   concentration gradient, with carriers and energy expenditure.
4. **Endocytosis** — large molecules engulfed by the cell membrane and released
   intracellularly.

**Bioavailability**: the fraction of administered drug that reaches systemic
circulation (rate and extent). Example: if 100 mg administered orally and 70 mg
absorbed unchanged, bioavailability = 0.7 (70%).

Determined by comparing plasma levels after a given route (e.g. oral) with plasma
levels achieved by IV injection. Important for calculating dosages for
non-intravenous routes.

Diagram/formula (p.18): BA = (AUC~po~ / AUC~iv~) × 100. GI lumen → GI wall (CYP3A4)
→ portal vein → liver → systemic circulation, with loss possible at each step
(metabolism/P-gp excretion into GI lumen at the wall; metabolism/biliary excretion
at the liver). Worked example: **propranolol BA = 26 ± 10%** (↑ BA with cirrhosis).

Factors that influence bioavailability:
1. **First-pass hepatic metabolism** — if rapidly metabolized by the liver, less
   unchanged drug reaches systemic circulation.
2. **Solubility of the drug** — very hydrophilic drugs poorly absorbed (can't cross
   lipid-rich membranes); extremely lipophilic drugs also poorly absorbed
   (insoluble in aqueous body fluids, can't reach cell surfaces). To be readily
   absorbed a drug must be largely lipophilic but have some aqueous solubility —
   one reason many drugs are weak acids or weak bases.
3. **Chemical instability** — e.g. penicillin G unstable at gastric pH; insulin
   destroyed by GI degradative enzymes.
4. **Nature of the drug formulation** — particle size, salt form, enteric coatings,
   excipients can alter ease of dissolution and rate of absorption.

### 2.2 Distribution {#ch02-2}

**Distribution**: the reversible transfer of drugs between body fluid compartments.

Factors affecting drug distribution (delivery from plasma to interstitium): (A)
cardiac output and regional blood flow, (B) capillary permeability, (C) tissue
volume, (D) degree of binding to plasma and tissue proteins, (E) lipophilicity.

Factors affecting capillary permeability: (1) capillary structure; (2) drug
structure — hydrophobic drugs readily cross most biologic membranes; hydrophilic
drugs do not readily penetrate cell membranes and must go through slit junctions.

Binding of drugs to proteins:
1. **Plasma protein binding** (e.g. albumin) — reversible; sequesters drugs in a
   nondiffusible form and slows transfer out of the vascular compartment.
2. **Tissue protein binding** — many drugs accumulate in tissues (binding to
   lipids, proteins, or nucleic acids), giving higher tissue than
   extracellular-fluid/blood concentration → prolongs drug action, can cause local
   drug toxicity.

**Volume of distribution (Vd)** ("apparent volume of distribution"): the
distribution of a medication between plasma and the rest of the body, or the
apparent/hypothetical fluid volume into which a drug is homogeneously distributed.
Vd is a property of the drug, determined by the manufacturer.

Water compartments in the body (three functionally distinct compartments — plasma,
interstitial fluid, intracellular fluid), for a reference 70 kg person:
- Total body water: 60% of body weight = 60% × 70 kg = **42 liters**
  - Intracellular fluid: 40% of body weight = **28 liters**
  - Extracellular fluid: 20% of body weight = **14 liters**
    - Plasma: 6% of body weight = **4 liters**
    - Interstitial volume: 14% of body weight = **10 liters**

Drug distribution by molecular/binding properties, mapped onto these compartments:
- **Plasma compartment**: large molecular weight or extensive plasma-protein
  binding — drug trapped in plasma — **Vd = 4 L**.
- **Extracellular fluid**: low molecular weight, hydrophilic — moves through
  fenestrae into interstitial fluid but cannot cross cell membranes into
  intracellular fluid — sum of plasma + interstitial water — **Vd = 14 L**.
- **Total body water**: low molecular weight, hydrophobic — moves into interstitial
  and intracellular fluid — **Vd = 42 L**.

Determination/use of Vd: useful to compare drug distribution with body water
compartment volumes; useful for calculating a loading dose. If Vd is big, the drug
is more diluted in blood plasma than it "should" be, meaning more is distributed in
tissue (not plasma).

Plasma concentration vs. time after rapid IV injection shows a **distribution
phase** followed by an **elimination phase**; most drugs show an exponential
decrease in concentration during the elimination phase. On a log-plasma-concentration
plot, extrapolating back to time zero gives **C₀**, the hypothetical concentration
if distribution had been instantaneous. **Half-life (t½)** — the time to reduce
plasma drug concentration by half — **t½ = 0.69 × Vd / CL**.

Effect of Vd on half-life: Vd has an important influence on half-life, since drug
elimination depends on the amount of drug delivered to organs of metabolism per unit
time. A large Vd means most of the drug is in the extraplasmic space and unavailable
to excretory organs. Any factor that increases Vd can increase the half-life and
extend duration of action. Delivery of drug to organs of elimination depends on
blood flow and the fraction of drug in plasma.

### 2.3 Metabolism (biotransformation) {#ch02-3}

Drug elimination has two major components: **biotransformation/metabolism** (the
drug is chemically converted to a metabolite) and **excretion** (removal of the
intact drug). Elimination begins as soon as the drug enters the body. Three major
elimination routes: (1) hepatic metabolism, (2) excretion into bile, (3) excretion
into urine.

Elimination causes plasma concentration to decrease exponentially — a constant
fraction is eliminated per unit time. Metabolism increases polarity, allowing
elimination. **Clearance (CL)** estimates the amount of drug cleared from the body
per unit time. Elimination kinetics: (1) **first-order** — most drugs; (2)
**zero-order (non-linear)** — e.g. aspirin at high doses. Drug half-life is often
used to measure CL, since Vd is roughly constant for many drugs.

Drug metabolism usually converts lipid-soluble, un-ionized compounds into
water-soluble, ionized compounds for excretion. Main site: **liver**. Others: GI
tract, kidney, blood, placenta. Outcomes: inactivation, activation (active drug →
active metabolite).

Two general sets of reactions:
- **Phase I** (lipophilic substrates) — introduces or unmasks a polar functional
  group (e.g. –OH or –NH₂), converting lipophilic molecules into more polar ones.
  Pharmacologic activity after Phase I may increase, decrease, or stay unchanged.
  Subtypes (p.41 of the deck):
  1. **Oxidation** (adds O), typically simple addition of O or hydroxylation (adds H
     and O); mediated predominantly by microsomal endoplasmic-reticulum cytochrome
     P450 liver enzymes (kidney and nervous-tissue enzymes can also oxidize).
  2. **Reduction** (gain of electron or H); mediated by P450 enzymes.
  3. **Hydrolysis** (addition of water); performed by hydrolytic enzymes called
     plasma esterases, e.g. plasma cholinesterase.

  **a. P450-mediated Phase I reactions** — catalyzed by the cytochrome P450 system
  (microsomal mixed-function oxidases); most common reaction is a monooxygenase
  reaction (one O atom inserted into the substrate RH, the other reduced to water).
  - *Nomenclature*: enzymes designated **CYP**, followed by a number for the family,
    a capital letter for the subfamily, another numeral for the specific gene/enzyme
    — e.g. **CYP3A4**.
  - *Specificity*: many P450 isoforms; can modify many structurally diverse
    substrates; one drug may be a substrate for more than one isozyme. Four
    isozymes responsible for the vast majority of P450-catalyzed reactions:
    **CYP3A4/5, CYP2D6, CYP2C8/9, CYP1A2**. Considerable CYP3A4 is found in
    intestinal mucosa (responsible for first-pass metabolism).
  - *Genetic variability*: P450 enzymes show considerable genetic variability among
    individuals and racial groups; variations can alter a drug's efficacy and
    adverse-event risk.
    - Example 1: **CYP2D6** exhibits genetic polymorphism. Patients lacking CYP2D6
      cannot benefit from codeine's analgesic effect (codeine is activated by
      O-demethylation via CYP2D6). Polymorphism is partly racially determined: 5–10%
      of European Caucasians, 2% of Southeast Asians.
    - Example 2: **CYP2C19** — clopidogrel is a CYP2C19 substrate; poor CYP2C19
      metabolizers have a higher incidence of cardiovascular events (stroke, MI) on
      clopidogrel.
  - *Inducers*: enzyme synthesis initiated within 24 h of exposure, increasing over
    3–5 days; effect decreases over 1–3 weeks after the inducer is discontinued.
    Environmental factors/xenobiotics: cigarette smoking, high-protein diet,
    ethanol, insecticide exposure (DDT, Lindane), PCBs. Certain drugs: barbiturates,
    phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampicin, dexamethasone.
    Consequences of increasing drug metabolism: (1) decreased plasma drug
    concentration, (2) decreased drug activity if the metabolite is inactive, (3)
    increased drug activity if the metabolite is active, (4) decreased therapeutic
    effect.
  - *Inhibitors*: inhibition of CYP isoenzyme activity is an important source of
    drug interactions — through competition for the same isoenzyme, or by
    inhibiting reactions for which the inhibitor is not itself a substrate (e.g.
    ketoconazole). Important CYP inhibitor drugs: **erythromycin, ketoconazole,
    ritonavir, cimetidine**. Natural substances can inhibit too — grapefruit/juice
    inhibits CYP3A4. Example CYP3A4 substrates: nifedipine (CCB), clarithromycin
    (macrolide), simvastatin (lipid-lowering agent).
    Example: **warfarin** biotransformation may be inhibited by **omeprazole**
    (a potent inhibitor of three CYP isozymes responsible for warfarin metabolism)
    — co-administration raises plasma warfarin, increasing bleeding/hemorrhage risk.
    Similarly, co-administration of the inhibitor **cimetidine** raises steady-state
    warfarin concentration and anticoagulant effect; the inducer **phenobarbitone**
    has the opposite effect.
  - **b. Phase I reactions not involving P450**: amine oxidation (e.g. oxidation of
    catecholamines or histamine), alcohol dehydrogenation (e.g. ethanol oxidation),
    esterases (e.g. pravastatin metabolism in liver), hydrolysis (e.g. of procaine).
- **Phase II** (conjugation with an endogenous molecule; hydrophilic) — if the
  Phase I metabolite is sufficiently polar it can be excreted renally directly;
  otherwise (many Phase I metabolites are too lipophilic) it is conjugated with an
  endogenous substrate — glucuronic acid, sulfuric acid, acetic acid, or an amino
  acid — giving polar, more water-soluble compounds, most often therapeutically
  inactive. Notable exception: **morphine-6-glucuronide**, more potent than
  morphine.
  - **Glucuronidation** — the most common and most important Phase II reaction.
    Neonates are deficient in this conjugating system. **Chloramphenicol** is
    inactivated by glucuronic-acid conjugation; deficient conjugation in neonates
    contributes to **gray baby syndrome**. Also relevant to bilirubin conjugation.
  - Drugs already possessing an –OH, –NH₂, or –COOH group may enter Phase II
    directly without prior Phase I metabolism; the resulting highly polar
    conjugates may be excreted by the kidney or in bile.
  - **Reversal of phase order**: not all drugs undergo Phase I then Phase II in that
    order — e.g. **isoniazid** is first acetylated (Phase II) then hydrolyzed to
    isonicotinic acid (Phase I).

Outcomes of metabolism: (1) abolishes activity, terminates drug action; (2) can
promote activity — prodrug, e.g. acetylsalicylate; (3) no change in activity, e.g.
diazepam → nordiazepam; (4) produces toxic metabolites, e.g. paracetamol.

### 2.4 Excretion {#ch02-4}

Main routes of excretion: (1) renal, (2) biliary. Other routes: exhaled air,
salivary, sweat, milk, tears.

**Renal excretion** — the kidney is the main excretory organ; the nephron is its
functional unit. Nephron structure (diagram): Bowman's capsule (glomerular —
filtration of smaller molecules) → proximal tubule (active secretion of weak
electrolytes, especially acids, and reabsorption of water) → loop of Henle
(reabsorption of water) → distal tubule (passive transfer of lipid-soluble drugs and
reabsorption of water) → urine.

Three major processes of renal drug elimination:
1. **Glomerular filtration** — normal GFR = 125 mL/min. Passage into the glomerular
   filtrate is influenced by GFR and protein binding of the drug; lipophilicity and
   pH have no effect here.
2. **Proximal tubular secretion** — occurs via active transport: one carrier for
   anions (e.g. deprotonated weak acids, A⁻), one for cations (e.g. protonated weak
   bases, BH⁺). Low specificity, can transport many compounds; competition between
   drugs for these carriers can occur within a transport system (e.g. penicillins
   and probenecid). Note: premature infants/neonates have incompletely developed
   tubular secretory mechanisms and may retain certain drugs in the glomerular
   filtrate.
3. **Distal tubular reabsorption** — passive reabsorption of lipid-soluble,
   uncharged drugs. Urine pH affects reabsorption: increasing the ionized fraction
   decreases back-diffusion and increases clearance of an undesirable drug
   ("ion trapping"). Acidifying the urine increases elimination of weak bases;
   alkalinizing the urine increases elimination of weak acids.
   - Example: an overdose of **phenobarbital or acetylsalicylic acid** (weak acids)
     can be treated with bicarbonate, which alkalinizes the urine and enhances
     ionized-drug excretion.
   - Example: an overdose of **amphetamine** (weak base) can be treated with NH₄Cl,
     which acidifies the urine, increases the protonated form (BH⁺), and enhances
     renal excretion.

Clearance by other routes: intestines, bile, lungs, milk (nursing mothers). Feces
mainly eliminate unabsorbed orally-ingested drugs and drugs secreted into the
intestine/bile. Lungs mainly eliminate anesthetic gases (e.g. halothane,
isoflurane).

**Total body clearance (CL~total~)** = sum of clearances from all
drug-metabolizing/eliminating organs: CL~total~ = CL~hepatic~ + CL~renal~ +
CL~pulmonary~ + CL~other~. CL~total~ and t½ are used to optimize drug therapy and
minimize toxicity.

Factors lowering renal drug clearance (dose reduction needed): elderly and newborn;
women (−20% vs. men); kidney and heart disease; patients taking drugs that block
secretion (aspirin, probenecid, cimetidine).

Half-life is increased by: (1) diminished renal or hepatic blood flow (cardiogenic
shock, heart failure, hemorrhage); (2) decreased ability to extract drug from plasma
(renal disease); (3) decreased metabolism (another drug inhibiting biotransformation,
or hepatic insufficiency, e.g. cirrhosis) — lower doses or less frequent dosing may
be required.

Half-life may decrease with: (1) increased hepatic blood flow, (2) decreased protein
binding, (3) increased metabolism — higher doses or more frequent dosing may be
required.

### 2.5 Dosage regimens {#ch02-5}

A **dosage regimen** is the manner in which a drug is taken. An optimal regimen
maximizes benefit and minimizes adverse effects. It is administered by continuous
infusion or in fixed intervals of time and dose, and depends on patient/drug factors
including how rapidly a **steady state** must be achieved (steady state = rate of
administration equals rate of elimination). The regimen is refined toward the
optimal dosage regimen.

Drug administration: single dose; continuous administration (IV infusion, or
fixed-dose/fixed-time-interval regimens). With IV infusion or oral
fixed-dose/fixed-time regimens, the drug accumulates until steady state; at steady
state, plasma/tissue levels remain constant with IV infusion but fluctuate around a
mean with oral fixed dosing.

Following initiation of IV infusion, plasma concentration rises until the
elimination rate balances the input rate (steady state); concentration then remains
constant (first-order elimination). The **steady-state concentration (Css)** is
directly proportional to the infusion rate and inversely proportional to the drug's
clearance — hepatic or renal disease can increase Css; increased metabolism
decreases Css.

**Fixed-dose/fixed-time-interval regimens** are more convenient than continuous
infusion but cause fluctuations in drug levels (multiple IV injections or multiple
oral doses).
- Multiple oral administrations: plasma concentration is influenced by rate of
  absorption and rate of elimination (absorption may be slow).
- Multiple IV injections: with repeated regular dosing, plasma concentration
  increases until steady state; using smaller doses at shorter intervals does not
  change the rate at which steady state is approached.

Optimization of dose: goal is to achieve/maintain concentration within the
therapeutic window while minimizing toxicity/side effects. Drugs with a small
therapeutic window (e.g. digoxin, warfarin) require caution in regimen selection and
may need drug-level monitoring. Regimens are administered as a **maintenance dose**
with or without a **loading dose**.

- **Maintenance dose** — maintains plasma concentration within a specified range
  (Css) over long-term therapy.
- **Loading dose** — a higher single dose or series of doses given to reach the
  target plasma level rapidly; may carry a higher toxicity risk. Useful for drugs
  with a relatively long t½.
- Ideal dosing plan is based on: minimum therapeutic level, minimum toxic
  concentration, clearance, and Vd.
  - Loading dose = (Vd) × (desired Css) / F
  - For IV: Loading dose = (Vd) × (desired Css)

Diagram: accumulation of orally-administered drug without a loading dose vs. with a
single oral loading dose at t=0 — with a loading dose, plasma concentration jumps
near the eventual steady-state band immediately; without it, concentration climbs
gradually over several elimination half-lives before reaching the same band.

**Therapeutic window**: the safe range between minimum therapeutic concentration and
minimum toxic concentration of a drug; used to determine the acceptable range of
plasma levels when designing a dosing regimen. On a concentration-vs-time curve this
appears as a band between "desired response" and "adverse response," with onset of
effect, peak effect, and duration of action marked along the curve; below the band
is "subtherapeutic," above it is "side effects."

## Ch. 3 — Pharmacodynamics {#ch03}

Pharmacodynamics: "what drugs do to the body" — mechanism of action.

### 3.1 Mechanism of drug action {#ch03-1}

Most drugs interact with a target biomolecule (usually a protein), located
extracellularly, on the cell (membrane), or intracellularly. Target types: enzymes,
ion channels, neurotransmitter transport systems, nucleic acids, receptors.

**Receptor-mediated action** — drugs produce effects by interacting with a chemical
compartment of the living organism (the receptor). Receptors are macromolecules,
mostly proteins, present on the cell surface, in the cytoplasm, or in the nucleus.

**Receptor**: a macromolecular component of a cell with which a drug interacts to
produce a response. Drug–receptor interactions:
- Mimic the actions of a neurotransmitter at the same site (**agonist**, like an
  endogenous molecule).
- Bind a nearby site and affect neurotransmitter binding (agonist/antagonist).
- Block the actions of a neurotransmitter at the same site (**antagonist**).

### 3.2 Types of receptors {#ch03-2}

1. **Membrane receptors**
   1. Ion channels (ligand-gated ion channels)
   2. G-protein coupled receptors
   3. Enzyme-linked receptors
2. **Intracellular receptors**

**1. Ligand-gated ion channels** — activity regulated by ligand binding to the
channel. Response is very rapid, lasting only a few milliseconds. Mediates diverse
functions: neurotransmission, cardiac or skeletal muscle contraction.

**2. G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs)** — seven transmembrane domains,
extracellular ligand-binding site, intracellular coupling to G proteins (Gs, Gi, and
others) with three subunits (α, binding GTP; β; γ). Response usually lasts several
seconds to minutes. (Figure: the G-protein-coupled adenylyl cyclase–cAMP system.)

**3. Enzyme-linked receptors** — form dimers or multisubunit complexes. Activation
increases cytoplasmic enzyme activity, e.g. tyrosine kinase; the activated receptor
phosphorylates tyrosine residues on itself and then other specific proteins,
modifying target-protein structure. Responses last minutes to hours. Control
metabolism, growth, and differentiation. Most common enzyme-linked receptors:
epidermal growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor, atrial natriuretic peptide,
insulin — these have tyrosine kinase activity as part of their structure.

Worked example — the **insulin receptor** (enzyme-linked receptor mechanism,
diagram): (1) insulin binding activates receptor tyrosine kinase activity in the
intracellular domain of the β subunit of the (inactive → active) insulin receptor;
(2) tyrosine residues of the β subunit are auto-phosphorylated; (3) the receptor
tyrosine kinase phosphorylates other proteins, e.g. insulin receptor substrates
(IRS); (4) phosphorylated IRSs promote activation of other protein kinases and
phosphatases → activation of multiple signaling pathways → biologic effects of
insulin.

**4. Intracellular receptors** — primary targets are transcription factors. The
activated ligand–receptor complex migrates/translocates to the nucleus, binds
specific DNA sequences, and regulates gene expression. Time course is much longer:
onset 30 minutes or more, duration of response hours to days.

Worked example — **steroid hormone** mechanism (diagram): (1) the steroid hormone
diffuses through the plasma membrane and binds an intracellular receptor protein,
forming a receptor–hormone complex; (2) the receptor–hormone complex enters the
nucleus; (3) it binds a hormone response element (a specific DNA sequence); (4)
binding initiates transcription of the gene to mRNA; (5) the mRNA directs protein
synthesis (new protein).

### 3.3 Drug–receptor binding and dose-response {#ch03-3}

D + R ⇌ DR complex.
- **Affinity**: measure of the propensity of a drug to bind its receptor — the
  attractiveness of drug and receptor. Covalent bonds are stable and essentially
  irreversible; electrostatic bonds may be strong or weak, but are usually
  reversible.
- **Efficacy** (or intrinsic activity): the ability of a bound drug to produce an
  effect.
- **Potency**: the amount of drug necessary to produce an effect of a given
  magnitude.

**Dose-response curves**:
- **Graded dose-response curve** — response intensity increases with dose;
  measurable on a continuous scale.
- **Quantal dose-response curve** — for drugs eliciting an all-or-none response
  (there either is or isn't a response), e.g. presence/absence of epileptic
  seizures. (Illustrated by a bell-shaped frequency-of-response-vs-dose histogram.)

**Graded dose-response relations**:
- **Potency** — amount of drug needed to produce an effect of a given magnitude;
  refers to the concentration (**EC50**) or dose (**ED50**) needed to produce 50% of
  the drug's maximal effect.
- **Efficacy** (magnitude of effect) — the maximal response; references the
  response to the drug. Clinical effectiveness depends on maximal efficacy, not on
  potency.

Worked example (Figure 2.8): Drug A is more potent than Drug B, but both show the
same efficacy (same maximal biologic effect, different EC50); Drug C shows lower
potency *and* lower efficacy than Drugs A and B.

**Log dose scale**: transforms the hyperbolic dose-response curve into a sigmoid
(nearly a straight line); the dose-response relation becomes linear, which is
easier to interpret and analyze mathematically, and lets one choose an optimized
dose (e.g. EC50, EC80) for mechanistic studies.

Concepts on the log-dose/response curve: **Threshold** — dose producing a
just-noticeable effect. **ED50** — dose producing 50% of maximal response. **EC50**
— concentration producing 50% of maximal response. **Ceiling** — lowest dose
producing a maximal effect.

### 3.4 Classification of drugs by drug-receptor interaction {#ch03-4}

**Agonist drugs** — two types:
- **Full agonist** — binds a receptor and produces a maximal biologic response
  (efficacy) mimicking the endogenous ligand's response.
- **Partial agonist** — cannot produce a full response no matter how high the dose;
  efficacy (intrinsic activity) greater than zero but less than a full agonist;
  affinity may be greater than, less than, or equivalent to a full agonist's; has
  both agonist and antagonist properties (may act as an antagonist of a full
  agonist).

**Antagonist drug** — binds receptors but cannot initiate a cellular response;
prevents agonists from producing a response. Antagonists maintain the
active-inactive equilibrium. Two types (as stated in the source):
- **Competitive** — antagonist binds the same site as the agonist in a reversible
  manner.
- **Noncompetitive** — antagonist binds the same site as the agonist irreversibly.

  *[Note: a worked diagram elsewhere in the same deck illustrates competitive
  inhibition (isoproterenol agonist + propranolol antagonist on heart rate) as a
  parallel rightward shift of the dose-response curve with no change in maximum —
  higher propranolol dose shifts EC50 further right; and noncompetitive inhibition
  (norepinephrine + phenoxybenzamine on blood pressure) as a decreased maximum
  response with no change in EC50. The diagram does not state that the
  noncompetitive antagonist binds the *same site*; this is presented as written,
  without reconciling the two descriptions.]*

**Inverse agonist** — a drug that binds a receptor to produce an effect opposite
that of an agonist; stabilizes the receptor in its inactive state.

**Effectiveness, toxicity, lethality**:
- **ED50** — median effective dose; dose at which 50% of the population/sample
  manifests a given effect.
- **TD50** — median toxic dose; dose at which 50% of the population manifests a
  given toxic effect.
- **LD50** — median lethal dose; dose that kills 50% of subjects.
- **Therapeutic Index (TI) = TD50 or LD50 / ED50.**

Diagram (Drug Safety – Therapeutic Index): on a %-responding vs. log[drug] plot, the
therapeutic-effect curve (ED50) sits to the left of the toxic-side-effect curve
(TD50); TI = TD50/ED50; the slope of the toxic curve "can" be steep.

Worked numeric example (Figure, Therapeutic Index): hypnosis curve (ED50 = 100 mg)
vs. death curve (LD50 = 400 mg) — **Therapeutic Index = LD50/ED50 = 400/100 = 4.**
(Same figure also marks LD1 = 200 mg and ED99 between the two curves.)

**Unusual responses** — definitions: **Idiosyncratic response** (unusual response),
**Hyporeactive** (less than normal response), **Hyperreactive** (more than normal
response), **Hypersensitivity** (allergic or other immunological reaction),
**Tolerance** (decreased response with continued administration), **Tachyphylaxis**
(rapidly developing tolerance).

## Ch. 4 — Principles of Antimicrobial Therapy and Cell Wall Inhibitors {#ch04}

Course scope, as outlined in this deck: antibacterial agents (A. cell wall
inhibitors, B. protein synthesis inhibitors, C. antimetabolites), antifungal
agents, antiviral agents.

### 4.1 Principles of antimicrobial therapy {#ch04-1}

Infection-associated factors: age (young and elderly), exposure to pathogen
(bacteria, virus, fungi), disruption of normal barriers, decreased immunological
defense, impaired circulation (diabetes), poor nutritional state.

**Antibiotics**: medications used to treat bacterial infections. Before starting
therapy, suspected infection sites should be cultured to identify the causative
organism and its antibiotic susceptibilities.

Principles of therapy:
- **Pathogen-directed therapy** — drug given after bacterial identification and
  susceptibility testing.
- **Empiric therapy** — treatment of an infection before specific culture
  information has been reported/obtained. Most patients require empiric therapy.
- **Broad-spectrum therapy** may be needed initially for serious infections when
  the organism is unknown, or when the infection site makes polymicrobial infection
  likely.
- **Prophylactic therapy** — antibiotics to prevent an infection, e.g. in
  intra-abdominal surgery.

Selection of antimicrobial agents depends on: (1) the organism's susceptibility to a
particular agent, (2) site of infection, (3) patient factors, (4) safety and
efficacy of the drug, (5) cost of therapy.

Patient factors considered: immune system; renal and hepatic dysfunction; poor
perfusion, age; pregnancy and lactation.

Route of administration: oral route chosen for mild infections and favorable for
outpatients; parenteral route used for more serious infections or when the agent of
choice has poor GI absorption.

Effect of site of infection — the blood–brain barrier: penetration/concentration of
an antibacterial agent in the CSF is particularly influenced by lipid solubility,
molecular weight, protein binding, and susceptibility to transporters or efflux
pumps.

**Bacteriostatic vs. bactericidal**: bacteriostatic drugs arrest bacterial growth and
replication, which will eventually lead to bacterial death; bactericidal drugs kill
bacteria at drug serum levels achievable in the patient.

- **Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)** (for bacteriostatic agents) — the
  lowest antibiotic concentration that inhibits bacterial growth.
- **Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)** (for bactericidal agents) — the
  minimum antibiotic concentration that kills the bacteria under investigation.

**Spectrum**: narrow spectrum — acts only on a limited group of microorganisms;
broad spectrum — acts on a wide variety of microbial species.

Complications of antibiotic therapy:
- **A. Hypersensitivity** — frequent reactions to antimicrobial drugs or their
  metabolic products, e.g. penicillins.
- **B. Direct toxicity** — high serum levels of certain antibiotics can directly
  affect host cellular processes, e.g. aminoglycosides causing ototoxicity.
- **C. Super-infections** — overgrowth of normal flora due to antibiotic treatment
  (broad-spectrum agents or drug combinations); can permit overgrowth of
  opportunistic organisms, especially fungi or resistant bacteria.

**General mechanisms of antibiotic resistance**:
1. **Alteration of the target site** — one of the most problematic resistances
   worldwide is methicillin resistance among *Staphylococcus aureus*.
2. **Enzyme inactivation of the antibiotic** — β-lactam antibiotics (penicillins &
   cephalosporins) can be inactivated by β-lactamases.
3. **Active transport of the antibiotic out of the bacterial cell (efflux pumps)** —
   removes some antibiotics, e.g. tetracyclines, macrolides, quinolones.
4. **Decreased permeability of the bacterial cell wall to the antibiotic** —
   alteration in porin proteins that form membrane channels, e.g. resistance of
   *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* to a variety of penicillins and cephalosporins.

**Sites of antimicrobial action** (overview diagram): **cell wall synthesis** — beta
lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams), vancomycin,
bacitracin; **cell membrane** — polymyxins; **folate synthesis** — sulfonamides,
trimethoprim (PABA → DHFA → THFA pathway); **nucleic acid synthesis** — DNA gyrase
inhibited by quinolones, RNA polymerase inhibited by rifampin; **protein synthesis**
— 30S subunit targeted by tetracyclines and aminoglycosides, 50S subunit targeted by
macrolides, clindamycin, linezolid, chloramphenicol, streptogramins.

#### Penicillins {#ch04-2-1}

**Characteristics of the β-lactam ring**: inhibits transpeptidase; active against
rapidly growing organisms synthesizing a peptidoglycan cell wall; bactericidal,
time-dependent effect; substrate for β-lactamases; an unstable, acid-labile
structure; immunogenic breakdown products.

**Mechanism of action**:
- **Penicillin-binding proteins (PBP)** — bacterial enzymes involved in cell wall
  synthesis; alterations in these targets confer resistance to penicillins.
- **Inhibition of transpeptidase** — some PBPs catalyze the cross-linkages between
  peptidoglycan chains; penicillins inhibit this transpeptidase-catalyzed reaction.
- **Production of autolysins** — degradative enzymes that participate in normal
  remodeling of the bacterial cell wall.

Penicillin subgroups (deck's own list):
- Prototype: **Penicillin G** (acid resistant note applies to Penicillin V, see
  below).
- **Penicillin V** — acid resistant.
- Penicillinase-resistant ("antistaphylococcal"): **methicillin, oxacillin**.
- **Nafcillin**.
- "Broad spectrum": **amoxicillin, ampicillin**.
- "Antipseudomonas": **azlocillin, piperacillin**.
- Combinations: + clavulanic acid (+ β-lactamase inhibitor).

**Natural penicillins — Penicillin G and Penicillin V**:
- Antimicrobial activity: both share antimicrobial spectra for aerobic Gram-positive
  organisms, but Penicillin G is more active against *Neisseria* sp. and anaerobes.
  90% of staphylococci are resistant.
- Pharmacokinetics: acid labile (Penicillin V is more acid-stable/oral); widely
  distributed (inflamed bone and CSF); rapid renal excretion.

**Extended-spectrum penicillins — aminopenicillins (ampicillin, amoxicillin)**:
1. Antimicrobial activity — broad spectrum; do not work against β-lactamase
   producers (e.g. *Pseudomonas*, *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*); β-lactamase inhibitors
   (clavulanate, sulbactam) extend the spectrum.
2. Absorption — both acid resistant, but more amoxicillin is absorbed by the
   intestinal tract than ampicillin after oral dosing; food interferes with
   ampicillin absorption but not amoxicillin.
3. Excretion — renal (inhibited by probenecid); appears in breast milk.
4. Therapeutic uses — upper respiratory tract infections, otitis media,
   uncomplicated UTIs, acute bacterial meningitis in children, typhoid fever,
   prophylaxis by dentists in patients at high risk of bacterial endocarditis.

**β-lactamase inhibitor combinations** — clavulanic acid binds to and inhibits
β-lactamase, keeping it inactive. Named combination products in this deck:
- **Timentin®** — ticarcillin + clavulanic acid.
- **Augmentin® / Augmentin BID®** — (amoxicillin +) clavulanic acid, alongside
  ticarcillin material on the same slide.
- **Unasyn®** (injection) — ampicillin sodium + sulbactam sodium.
- **Tazocin®** — piperacillin (antipseudomonal penicillin) + tazobactam (inhibits
  bacterial β-lactamases).

**Mechanisms of resistance to penicillins**: (1) altered PBPs for penicillin binding
(e.g. methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*, MRSA); (2) enzymatic cleavage
of the β-lactam ring, preventing transpeptidase binding; (3) active transport out of
the cell (efflux pumps); (4) poor penetration into the cell (intrinsic resistance).

Selected clinical uses of penicillins: streptococci (pneumonia, meningitis,
pharyngitis, endocarditis, rheumatic fever); staphylococcal skin/wound infections;
meningococcus; syphilis; *Clostridia* (tetanus, gangrene); *H. influenzae*
(pneumonia); acute UTI (*E. coli*, *Pseudomonas*, enterococci).

Toxicity/contraindications: hypersensitivity reactions (uncommon); pain and sterile
inflammatory reactions at IM injection sites; large doses (>20 million IU/day) in
renal failure can cause lethargy, confusion, twitching, seizures; dizziness,
tinnitus, headache, hallucinations; pseudomembranous colitis due to *Clostridium
difficile* overgrowth.

#### Cephalosporins {#ch04-2-2}

Structurally similar to penicillins. Characteristics: inhibit bacterial cell wall
synthesis; absorption/distribution/elimination similar to penicillins. Adverse
reactions: hypersensitivity (1st and 2nd generation), thrombophlebitis,
nephrotoxicity. Incidence of resistance is lower than for penicillins.

**Generations of cephalosporins** (deck's own list, p.44):
- **First generation**: cephalothin, cephalexin, cephradine, cefazolin.
- **Second generation**: cefaclor, cefuroxime, cefoxitin.
- **Third generation**: cefotaxime, ceftriaxone (Rocephin®), ceftazidime, cefixime
  (orally).
- **Fourth generation**: cefepime, cefpirome.
- **Fifth generation**: ceftolozane, ceftaroline — for infections with
  gram-negative bacteria resistant to conventional antibiotics.

**Classification-and-spectrum trend** (diagram, distinct drug list): as the
1st, 2nd, and 3rd generations progress, sensitivity to gram-negative organisms
increases while sensitivity to gram-positive organisms decreases (β-lactamase
resistance moves the opposite way). The 4th and 5th generations show activity
towards both.

**Per-drug therapeutic notes** (Figure 29.13 — therapeutic advantages of some
clinically useful cephalosporins; orally-administered drugs noted, more useful
drugs in bold in the source figure):
- *First generation* — **Cefazolin**: parenteral, longer duration of action and
  similar spectrum to other 1st-generation drugs; penetrates well into bone.
  Cefadroxil (oral). **Cephalexin**: the prototype oral 1st-generation drug; twice
  daily, effective against pharyngitis.
- *Second generation* — **Cefuroxime sodium**: parenteral, longer half-life than
  similar agents; crosses the blood–brain barrier; used for community-acquired
  bronchitis/pneumonia in the elderly and immunocompromised patients. Cefuroxime
  axetil (oral): twice daily, well absorbed, active against β-lactamase-producing
  organisms.
- *Third generation* — Cefdinir, cefixime (oral, once daily). **Cefotaxime**:
  penetrates well into CSF. **Ceftazidime**: active against *Pseudomonas
  aeruginosa*. **Ceftriaxone**: longest half-life of any cephalosporin (6–8 hours),
  permitting once-daily dosing; high blood/CSF levels achievable; effective against
  genital, anal, and pharyngeal penicillin-resistant *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*;
  excreted in bile, may be used in renal insufficiency; good bone penetration.
- *Fourth generation* — **Cefepime**: active against *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
- *Advanced generation* — **Ceftaroline**: active against MRSA.

Cephalosporin PK note (diagram): administered IV/IM; most cephalosporins do not
penetrate the CSF, but third-generation agents achieve therapeutic CSF levels;
ceftriaxone appears in bile; mostly unchanged drug appears in urine.

Some uses for cephalosporins: hospital-acquired pneumonias — cefotaxime; meningitis
— cefotaxime, ceftriaxone; sepsis (initial Rx) — third and fourth generation
cephalosporins; gonorrhea — third generation; acute UTI.

#### Other cell wall/membrane inhibitors {#ch04-2-3}

**Newer β-lactams**: Imipenem, Aztreonam.

**Imipenem-cilastatin (Primaxin)** — a carbapenem. **Imipenem** binds to PBP-2,
causing cell lysis. **Cilastatin** is a peptidase inhibitor that blocks renal
degradation of imipenem. Broad spectrum; good coverage of hospital-acquired
gram-negative organisms including anaerobes. Allergic reactions. Administered
parenterally.

**New-generation carbapenems**:
- **Meropenem** — does not require co-administration of cilastatin; more convenient
  than imipenem (3×/day rather than 4×); administered as an IV bolus, whereas
  imipenem is given as a 20-minute to one-hour infusion; more gram-negative
  activity; effective for bacterial meningitis, pneumonia, UTI, gynecologic
  infection, peritonitis; higher bacterial response and lower adverse-event rates
  than imipenem.
- **Doripenem** — spectrum of activity very similar to meropenem; greater stability
  in solution allows prolonged infusions; less likely to produce seizures than other
  carbapenems.

**Aztreonam** — narrow spectrum, gram-negative; highly resistant to lactamases; few
serious adverse reactions; an alternative to aminoglycosides or 3rd-generation
cephalosporins; administered IV or IM; considered safe in patients allergic to
penicillins, cephalosporins, or carbapenems.

**Vancomycin**: prevents polymerization of peptidoglycan. Narrow spectrum —
*Staph.* & *Strep.*, *Enterococcus*, *Clostridium* sp. Ototoxicity. Clinical uses:
MRSA, methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus epidermidis* (MRSE) infections,
enterococcal infections, *Clostridium difficile* (alternate to metronidazole — not
absorbed orally).
- PK: IV administration; oral for *C. difficile*; inflammation allows penetration to
  meninges; 80–90% excreted by glomerular filtration; normal t½ = 6–10 hr, up to
  200 hr in renal failure.
- Mechanism of action (diagram): vancomycin — "the crucial drug of last resort" —
  inhibits peptidoglycan synthesis by binding **directly** to the D-Ala—D-Ala end of
  the peptide, forming a cap over the end of the chain that blocks cross-linking
  (transpeptidation and transglycosylation). Peptidoglycan that has completed
  cross-linking is resistant to osmotic pressure (cell survival); peptidoglycan
  blocked by vancomycin before cross-linking is susceptible to osmotic pressure
  (cell lysis).
- Toxicity/contraindications: **A. Red-man syndrome** (common) — erythematous or
  urticarial reaction to rapid infusion; mast cells release histamine because of
  vancomycin's toxicity (also called erythrodermatitis). **B. Oto- and
  nephrotoxicity** (uncommon to rare), due to excessively high concentrations.

**Bacitracin** — bacterial cell wall synthesis inhibitor; active against a wide
variety of gram-positive organisms; strictly used topically; nephrotoxicity.

**Others for resistant gram-positive organisms (MRSA, vancomycin-resistant
enterococci/VRE)**: (1) Daptomycin, (2) Fosfomycin, (3) Telavancin (transcribed in
source as "Telvancin").

**Cell membrane inhibitors — Polymyxins**: bind to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the
outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria; disrupt cell membrane integrity; leakage
of cell components and cell death. Systemic use causes nephrotoxicity and
neurotoxicity; use limited to patients with multidrug-resistant infections; careful
dosing and monitoring of adverse effects is important.

**Carbapenems** (Imipenem, Meropenem, Ertapenem): effect on microbes and

Parenteral administration. Drugs of choice for infections caused by *Enterobacter*.

**Non-beta-lactam cell wall synthesis inhibitors** (this deck's list): (1)

Bacitracin.

**Vancomycin — additional detail**: a tricyclic glycopeptide, effective against
multiple drug-resistant organisms such as MRSA and enterococci; there is concern
about the emergence of vancomycin resistance.
- Mode of action (this deck's phrasing): inhibits synthesis of bacterial cell wall
  phospholipids and inhibits peptidoglycan polymerization by binding to the
  D-Ala-D-Ala side chain of the precursor pentapeptide, preventing the
  transglycosylation step, weakening the cell wall and damaging the underlying cell
  membrane. Also described as inhibiting cell wall synthesis by inhibiting
  peptidoglycan synthetase. Diagrams (pp. 21–23 of this deck) illustrate the
  peptidoglycan lattice as alternating NAM/NAG (N-acetylmuramic
  acid/N-acetylglucosamine) units cross-linked by pentapeptide bridges, assembled
  via a membrane-embedded carrier lipid called **bactoprenol**, with teichoic acid
  and lipoteichoic acid anchored in the layer; vancomycin ("Van") binding is shown
  preventing formation of the peptide cross-bridge at that site, while unaffected
  sites ("A") complete normal cross-linking.
- Antibacterial spectrum: effective primarily against gram-positive organisms;
  lifesaving in MRSA, methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus epidermidis* (MRSE),
  and enterococcal infections. A spectrum figure in this deck lists: gram (+) cocci
  — *Staphylococcus aureus* (including methicillin-resistant strains),
  *Staphylococcus epidermidis*, *Streptococcus* groups A/B/C, *Streptococcus
  pneumoniae*, *Enterococcus faecalis*; gram (+) bacilli — *Listeria
  monocytogenes*; other — *Actinomyces*; anaerobic organisms — *Clostridium*
  species.
- Use restricted to: (1) treatment of serious infections caused by
  beta-lactam-resistant, gram-positive microorganisms, or (2) patients with
  gram-positive infections who have a serious allergy to beta-lactams. Oral
  vancomycin is limited to treatment of potentially life-threatening,
  antibiotic-associated colitis due to *C. difficile* or staphylococci.
- Treatment options for vancomycin-resistant organisms: daptomycin,
  quinupristin/dalfopristin, linezolid. Vancomycin acts synergistically with
  aminoglycosides, a combination usable in treating enterococcal endocarditis.
- Pharmacokinetics: usually given IV over an hour; can be given orally for
  pseudomembranous colitis. ~30% bound to serum proteins; appears in various body
  fluids, including CSF if inflammation is present. Excreted by the kidneys.
- Toxicity: **A. Red-man syndrome** (common) — erythematous or urticarial reaction
  to rapid infusion; mast cells release histamine because of vancomycin's
  toxicity. **B. Ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity** (uncommon to rare), due to
  excessively high concentrations.
- A referenced chart (source: Center for Disease Dynamics, Economics & Policy,
  using CDC data) shows oral vancomycin prescriptions per 100,000 population and
  *C. difficile* mortality per 1,000,000 population both rising in the United
  States from 2000–2007, with a brief production shortage of vancomycin noted
  around 2002–2003.

## Ch. 5 — Protein Synthesis Inhibitors {#ch05}

Classes covered: (1) Tetracyclines, (2) Glycylcyclines, (3) Aminoglycosides, (4)
Macrolides/Ketolides, (5) Others.

### 5.1 Macrolides {#ch05-1}

Drugs pictured/named: **Erythromycin, Clarithromycin, Azithromycin**.

**Mechanism of action**: inhibit protein synthesis by irreversibly binding to the
50S ribosomal subunit; inhibit translocation of peptidyl tRNA. Bacteriostatic (can
be bactericidal at high concentrations against susceptible bacteria). Used as an
alternative to penicillin in patients with β-lactam antibiotic (allergy, implied).

**Pharmacokinetics**:
- All are oral drugs; erythromycin and azithromycin also have IV preparations.
- Absorption: erythromycin must be coated to avoid destruction by gastric acid;
  azithromycin and clarithromycin are acid-stable. Food delays absorption of
  erythromycin and azithromycin but can increase absorption of clarithromycin.
- Distribution: large Vd; readily diffuses into intracellular fluids except CSF.
  Erythromycin diffuses into prostatic fluid. Macrolides concentrate inside
  macrophages and neutrophils, and in liver tissue.
- Excretion: kidney for clarithromycin; liver for erythromycin and azithromycin.
  Azithromycin has the longest t½ (40 hours).

**Erythromycin**: liver metabolism, t½ = 1.6 hr. Strong inhibitor of CYP3A4,
reducing metabolism/elimination of numerous drugs and raising their plasma
concentration (some interactions life-threatening) — e.g. midazolam, quinidine,
warfarin, digoxin.
- Adverse effects: ~30% GI (cramps, abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea —
  dose-related, may be reduced with food); hepatic effects/cholestatic hepatotoxicity
  (much more common with pre-existing liver dysfunction — contraindicated in liver
  dysfunction); ototoxicity (most common with high-dose parenteral erythromycin,
  usually reversible); QTc prolongation.

**Clarithromycin**: more acid stable than erythromycin; longer t½ (4 hr). Better
tolerated than erythromycin (lower GI side effects, still occur); taste
disturbances (more common at higher doses); drug interaction profile similar to
erythromycin.

**Azithromycin**: more acid stable than erythromycin; longer t½ (35–40 hr); the
least likely of the three macrolides to inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes.
Extraordinarily well tolerated; nausea/vomiting, headache in <1% of patients; very
few drug interactions — a major advantage over erythromycin and clarithromycin.

**Macrolide resistance mechanisms**:
- **Target site alteration** — methylation of the 23S rRNA of the 50S ribosomal
  subunit (MLS resistance); methylation affects overlapping ribosomal targets,
  conferring resistance to all macrolides, lincosamides, and streptogramins
  (MLSB); high-level resistance (MIC > 64 µg/mL).
- **Enzymatic inactivation** — of erythromycin, by esterases or phosphotransferases.

Therapeutic uses of macrolides: (A) *Mycoplasma* infections, (B) *Chlamydia*
infections, (C) staph and strep infections (safe alternative for patients sensitive
to β-lactams), (D) GI infections — *Campylobacter* gastroenteritis, *Helicobacter
pylori* infections.

What clarithromycin and azithromycin add to spectrum: more potent against selected
pathogens — *H. influenzae*, *Helicobacter pylori*, selected *Mycobacteria* spp.
(*M. avium* complex, *M. fortuitum*, *M. chelonae*).

**Ketolides: Telithromycin** — a modification of the macrolide structure; overcomes
inducible MLSB and efflux resistance (but not constitutive resistance); acts by
binding the peptidyl transferase site of 23S rRNA of the 50S subunit; superior to
macrolides for some resistant gram-positives (e.g. *S. pneumoniae*); severe
hepatotoxicity limits its use.

**Lincosamides: Clindamycin** — clindamycin and lincomycin are both available, but
clindamycin is the most widely used (better absorption). Binds the same site as
macrolides (50S subunit). Resistance: decreased affinity of drug for the bacterial
50S ribosome; cross-resistance between clindamycin and erythromycin.

**Clindamycin PK**: well absorbed orally, bioavailability ~90%; available PO, IV,
IM, topical. Widely distributed; does not penetrate CSF; high concentrations in
bone and middle ear; concentrates within neutrophils. Extensively metabolized to
inactive forms, t½ = 2.7 hr. Excretion: bile, renal (10% excreted unchanged in
urine).

Clindamycin therapeutic uses: anaerobic infections except brain abscesses;
staphylococcal infections (in combination with penicillin); soft tissue infections;
drug of choice for anaerobic respiratory tract infections; osteomyelitis (with
ciprofloxacin); antiprotozoal agent; 2nd-line regimens for selected opportunistic
infections in AIDS patients (sulfa intolerant).

Clindamycin side effects: diarrhea (common); pseudomembranous colitis due to
overgrowth of toxin-producing *Clostridium difficile* (resistant to clindamycin) —
characterized by (bloody) diarrhea, fever, cramps, yellow-white plaques on colonic
mucosa; diagnosed by antigen detection of *C. difficile* toxin in stool; treatment —
stop clindamycin, give metronidazole or oral vancomycin, avoid antiperistalsis
agents.

### 5.2 Tetracyclines and glycylcyclines {#ch05-2}

**Tetracyclines: doxycycline, tetracycline, minocycline**. Broad spectrum; bind
metal ions (e.g. Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺); renal and hepatic elimination. Adverse
reactions: GI distress, nephrotoxicity, staining of teeth/bones, photosensitivity.

**Mechanism of action**: inhibit protein synthesis by reversibly binding to the 30S
ribosomal subunit; inhibit tRNA binding to mRNA. Also impair protein synthesis in
mammalian cells at high concentration. Bacteriostatic; broad spectrum.

**Mechanism of resistance**: lack of active transport of tetracyclines across the
bacterial cell membrane; efflux of tetracyclines from the microbe; enzymatic
inactivation. Resistance to one tetracycline does not mean resistance to all
tetracyclines.

**Tetracyclines: PK** — oral absorption variable, impaired by di- and trivalent
cations. Vd can be greater than body water. Minocycline and doxycycline achieve
therapeutic CSF concentration. T½ = 6–16 hr. Excretion: renal for tetracycline;
doxycycline and minocycline (doxycycline more than minocycline) eliminated
hepatically.

PK diagram: doxycycline and minocycline achieve therapeutic concentrations in CSF;
doxycycline glucuronide is excreted via bile; most tetracyclines are reabsorbed from
bile, metabolized to glucuronides, and excreted in urine.

**Tetracyclines: toxicity** — GI side effects common; deposition in teeth causes
permanent discoloration, demineralization, predisposition to decay — contraindicated
during tooth development (<8 yrs) and in pregnant women. Hepatotoxicity (rare but
potentially fatal). Phototoxicity (darkening of skin, sunburn on sun exposure).
Hypersensitivity reaction — rash, hives with itching, anaphylactic reaction
(decreased BP, increased HR, histamine release, etc.).

Effects on microbial agents: wide antimicrobial activity against gram-positive and
gram-negative bacteria, aerobic and anaerobic organisms. Activity ranking:
minocycline most active, then doxycycline, then tetracycline, then oxytetracycline
(least active). Uses: rickettsial infections, mycoplasma infections (atypical
pneumonia), chlamydia infections, acne vulgaris (doxycycline).

**Glycylcyclines — Tigecycline**: the first available member, a derivative of
minocycline; indicated for complicated skin and soft tissue infections. Developed to
overcome the recent emergence of tetracycline-class-resistant organisms that use
efflux and ribosomal protection for resistance.

### 5.3 Aminoglycosides {#ch05-3}

Bind to the 30S ribosomal subunit, inducing misreading of mRNA codons during
translation and inhibiting translocation. Bactericidal; killing is concentration
dependent. Postantibiotic effect (PAE). Named drugs: **gentamicin, tobramycin,
amikacin, streptomycin, neomycin**.

Effect on microbes — spectrum of coverage is narrow: (1) work against gram-negative
aerobic bacteria; (2) work against gram-positive cocci if combined with a β-lactam
(synergistic effect); (3) selected agents active against mycobacteria — streptomycin
against *Mycobacteria tuberculosis*, amikacin against non-tuberculous mycobacteria.

**Resistance**: enzyme inactivation via aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes
(acetyltransferases, adenyltransferases, phosphotransferases); target alteration
(less common); primary resistance due to decreased uptake.

**Pharmacokinetics**: polar cations, poorly absorbed from the GI tract; IV or IM
preparation. Neomycin not given parenterally due to nephrotoxicity. Given topically
(skin infection), or orally before colorectal surgery and for hepatic
encephalopathy treatment. Tissue concentrations generally lower than serum;
concentrate in kidney and middle ear. Cleared by the kidneys unchanged; clearance
proportional to GFR. Contraindicated in pregnancy.

**Uses/PK/mechanism summary (diagram)**: routes — IM or IV, topical. Oral neomycin
before elective bowel surgery (not absorbed). Almost always used along with a
cell-wall synthesis inhibitor. Uses: pneumonia, MRSA, wide variety of gram-negative
and some gram-positive bacteria, upper respiratory tract procedures; endocarditis
treatment; bacteremia/sepsis (aerobes only); skin infections (topical); UTIs,
GI/GU procedures.
- Pharmacokinetics detail: **Vd = ECF (25% of body weight)**; adjust maintenance
  dosing based on [creatinine]; plasma monitoring necessary.
- Mechanism detail: irreversible inhibition of protein synthesis (30S), with
  O₂-dependent transport (bactericidal) — illustrated for gentamicin.
- Adverse effects: ototoxicity (reversible vestibular and irreversible auditory);
  nephrotoxicity (reversible); NMJ blockade (high dose); pregnancy category C
  (8th nerve).

### 5.4 Chloramphenicol {#ch05-4}

Broad spectrum, very active drug. Binds reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunit,
inhibiting protein synthesis at the peptidyl transferase reaction. Bacteriostatic.

Adverse reactions: gray baby syndrome; reversible, dose-dependent bone marrow
suppression; aplastic anemia.

Hematologic toxicity is the most important adverse effect — affects the
hematopoietic system in two ways: (1) a dose-related toxic effect presenting as
anemia, leukopenia, or thrombocytopenia; (2) a non-dose-related idiosyncratic
response manifesting as aplastic anemia, in many cases leading to fatal
pancytopenia.

**Gray baby syndrome**: fatal chloramphenicol toxicity that may develop in
neonates, especially premature babies, exposed to excessive drug doses. Usually
begins 2–9 days after treatment starts. Manifestations in the first 24 hours:
vomiting, refusal to suck, irregular/rapid respiration, abdominal distention,
periods of cyanosis, loose green stools. Progresses to an ashen-gray color and
hypothermia.

Clinical uses: restricted to life-threatening infections; used when other drugs
fail or when it is superior to all alternatives. Primary indications: typhoid
fever, topical treatment of eye infections. Leukocyte and differential count used to
monitor bone marrow function.

### 5.5 Streptogramins and oxazolidinones {#ch05-5}

**Streptogramin: Quinupristin/Dalfopristin** (30:70 ratio, IV) — inhibits protein
synthesis at two unique steps associated with the 50S ribosome; individually
bacteriostatic, bactericidal in combination. Broad activity against gram-positive
aerobes; very active against MRSA, VREF (*E. faecium*). Side effects:
hyperbilirubinemia; CYP3A4 inhibitor.

Use: infections due to the most problematic resistant gram-positive organisms —
MRSA, vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus faecium*; no activity against *E.
faecalis*. Use is now limited by the availability of linezolid, which has broader
spectrum, is better tolerated, and has oral bioavailability.

**Oxazolidinones: Linezolid** — a newer class of agent; broadly active against
gram-positives including MRSA, VRE; bacteriostatic against most organisms;
excellent (100%) oral bioavailability; generally well tolerated;
thrombocytopenia with prolonged therapy; weak MAOI activity (clinical significance
unclear).

Linezolid indications: infections due to resistant gram-positive organisms — MRSA,
vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus*. The only good oral option for therapy of VRE,
MRSA, or VRSA infections.

## Ch. 6 — Antimetabolites and DNA Synthesis Inhibitors {#ch06}

Covers folic acid antagonists and quinolones.

### 6.1 Folate synthesis inhibitors (sulfonamides, trimethoprim) {#ch06-1}

Enzymes requiring folate-derived cofactors are essential for purine and pyrimidine
synthesis. Many bacteria are impermeable to folic acid and other folates and must
therefore synthesize folate de novo.

**Antimetabolite antibiotics — history**: in 1932, sulfa drugs were noted to have
antibacterial properties — the first antibacterial agent used in the U.S.

**Full drug list (diagram)**:
- Inhibitors of folate synthesis: **Mafenide** (Sulfamylon), **Silver sulfadiazine**
  (Silvadene, SSD, Thermazene), **Sulfadiazine** (generic only), **Sulfasalazine**
  (Azulfidine).
- Inhibitors of folate reduction: **Pyrimethamine** (Daraprim), **Trimethoprim**
  (Primsol, Trimpex).
- Combination of inhibitors of folate synthesis and reduction: **Cotrimoxazole**
  (trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole) (Bactrim, Septra).

**Sulfonamides: mechanism of action** — bacteriostatic; high concentration of PABA
inhibits sulfa drug activity (competitive inhibition of PABA incorporation into
folate synthesis).

**Sulfonamide classification by duration of action** (diagram), for treatment of
systemic infections:
- Short acting: sulfadiazine, sulfamethazine, sulfacetamide, sulfisoxazole,
  sulfamethizole.
- Intermediate acting: sulfamethoxazole, sulfamoxazole.
- Long acting: sulfadoxine, sulfamethopyrazine, sulfadimethoxine, sulformethoxine.
- Used for ulcerative colitis: sulfasalazine.
- Used topically: mafenide, silver sulfadiazine, sulfacetamide.

**Spectrum of activity**: not typically used alone for common bacterial infections
since bacteriostatic. Broad spectrum — many gram-positive and gram-negative
organisms; activity against some parasites (*Plasmodium* [malaria], *Toxoplasma
gondii*).

**Pharmacokinetics**: typically given orally, can be given IV (also topical and
suppository forms, per diagram). Sulfasalazine is not absorbed orally — reserved
for chronic inflammatory bowel disease. Distribution: well distributed, including
CSF; readily crosses the placenta and reaches fetal circulation (contraindicated in
pregnancy). Metabolism: liver (acetylation). Excretion: by the kidney — unchanged
drug and acetylated metabolites appear in urine; dose reduction required in renal
failure; may be eliminated in breast milk. Drug crosses the blood–brain barrier.

**Antimicrobial resistance**: widespread and irreversible; common in *Staph*,
*Strep*, *Enterobacteriaceae*, *Neisseria*; cross-resistance to all sulfas is
typical. Mechanisms: overproduction of PABA; altered
tetrahydropteroate synthetase with decreased affinity for sulfas (often plasmid
mediated); decreased cellular permeability to sulfa drugs.

**Toxicity and adverse events**: GI distress, rash (common); hemolytic anemia in
patients with G6PD deficiency; **kernicterus** in infants (competes for bilirubin
binding sites on albumin, increasing unconjugated bilirubin — CNS toxicity);
crystalluria and nephrotoxicity (adequate hydration and urine alkalinization can
prevent this); photosensitivity (dermatitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, epidermal
necrolysis); increases serum concentration of warfarin (increases INR) and
methotrexate.

**Trimethoprim** — acts sequentially in purine synthesis (relative to sulfonamides'
step).

**Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (Cotrimoxazole)** — synergistic effect.
- Trimethoprim pharmacology: typically oral; wide distribution including prostatic
  and vaginal fluids, and CSF; excretion mostly renal (80% unchanged).
- Spectrum: broad spectrum including MRSA.
- Mechanism of resistance (trimethoprim): overproduction of DHFR; production of a
  mutated DHFR resistant to trimethoprim (often plasmid mediated); resistance has
  been increasing — >20% of *E. coli* resistant in some regions.
- Toxicity: all the toxicities of sulfa, plus bone marrow suppression (megaloblastic
  anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia), reversed by folinic acid administration.
- Common uses: UTIs (decreased efficacy with increasing *E. coli* resistance),
  respiratory tract infections, *Pneumocystis* pneumonia in AIDS/transplant,
  sinusitis, otitis media. Concentrates in vaginal and prostatic fluids.

### 6.2 DNA synthesis inhibitors — quinolones {#ch06-2}

**DNA gyrase & DNA topoisomerase IV inhibitors** — gram-negative: gyrase;
gram-positive: topoisomerase IV.

**Quinolones — mechanism of action**: rapidly inhibits bacterial DNA synthesis. (1)
**DNA gyrase** (topoisomerase II), for gram-negative organisms — inhibition
prevents negative supercoiling in replicating DNA. (2) **Topoisomerase IV**, for
gram-positive organisms — inhibition prevents separation of DNA strands.

**Fluoroquinolones**: moxifloxacin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, ofloxacin,
norfloxacin. Moxifloxacin has higher affinity for topoisomerase IV (e.g.
gram-positive *S. pneumoniae*); ciprofloxacin has higher affinity for DNA gyrase
(e.g. gram-negative *P. aeruginosa*). Bactericidal; killing is concentration
dependent (AUC/MIC).

PK: excellent GI absorption giving serum levels comparable to IV dosing. High
volume of distribution, but CSF levels are less than serum; distributes to bone,
urine (except moxifloxacin), prostatic tissue. Elimination: most are cleared by the
kidney (adjust dose for renal patients); moxifloxacin is metabolized by the liver —
do not use in hepatic failure.

Spectrum of activity: gram negatives including *Pseudomonas*, where
cipro > levo > moxi; gram positives including streptococci, where
moxi > levo > cipro. Poor anaerobic activity. Excellent against atypical bacteria
(*Legionella*, *Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydia*).

Pharmacology of select quinolones:
- **Ciprofloxacin** (2nd) — particularly useful for infections caused by many
  *Enterobacteriaceae* and other gram-negative bacilli, e.g. traveler's diarrhea;
  the most potent fluoroquinolone for *P. aeruginosa*; also used as an alternative
  to aminoglycosides; may act synergistically with β-lactams; also of benefit in
  treating resistant tuberculosis. Not effective against serious infections caused
  by MRSA, enterococci, or pneumococci.
- **Levofloxacin** (3rd) — broad spectrum; usable for prostatitis due to *E. coli*
  and for STDs (including gonorrhea); used for a wide range of infections including
  skin infections and *S. pneumoniae* respiratory infections.
- **Moxifloxacin** (4th) — enhanced activity against gram-positive organisms (e.g.
  *S. pneumoniae*); excellent activity against many anaerobes; very poor activity
  against *P. aeruginosa*; does not concentrate in urine and is not indicated for
  UTIs.

Therapeutic uses of quinolones: urinary tract infections (except moxifloxacin),
prostatitis, STDs, gastrointestinal and abdominal infections, respiratory tract
infections, bone/joint/soft tissue infections.

Adverse effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea (most common); headache, dizziness;
nephrotoxicity (crystalluria at 3–4× normal doses); phototoxicity (use sunscreen);
QT prolongation. Contraindicated/avoided: children <18 years; pregnancy and
lactation; arrhythmic patients.

### 6.3 Urinary tract antiseptics/antimicrobials {#ch06-3}

**Methenamine** — mechanism: decomposes at acidic urine pH ≤5.5, producing
formaldehyde, which is toxic to most bacteria; bacteria do not develop resistance to
formaldehyde. Antibacterial spectrum: used for lower UTIs, not effective for upper
UTIs.

**Nitrofurantoin** — less commonly employed for UTIs because of its narrow
antimicrobial spectrum and toxicity. Sensitive bacteria reduce the drug to an active
agent that inhibits various enzymes and damages DNA. Spectrum: *E. coli* (effective),
ineffective against other gram-negative bacteria; gram-positive cocci. Adverse
effects: GI disturbances, acute pneumonitis, neurologic problems, pulmonary
fibrosis, hemolytic anemia in patients with G6PD deficiency. Contraindications:
renal impairment, pregnancy (3rd trimester).

A second slide on nitrofurantoin, from the gap-fill deck (`slides/2023
slides/Dr.Hussain/(7) Quinolone_Sulfonamides.pdf`, §6.4 below), gives the spectrum
slightly differently: *E. coli*, but other common urinary tract gram-negative
bacteria may be resistant; gram-positive cocci. Adverse effects there are listed as
GI disturbances and neurologic problems (pulmonary fibrosis and hemolytic anemia in
G6PD deficiency are not repeated on that slide). Both are given as written, without
merging into one adverse-effect list.

Urinary tract antiseptics are also described (same gap-fill deck) as a group: UTIs
are the most common problem seen by primary care physicians — *Escherichia coli*
causes 80% of uncomplicated upper and lower UTIs, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* is
the second most common cause. These agents (methenamine, nitrofurantoin, nalidixic
acid) do not achieve antibacterial levels in the circulation but work because they
concentrate in the urine.

### 6.4 Gap-fill: quinolones, sulfonamides, and antiprotozoal drugs (from `slides/2023 slides/Dr.Hussain/(7) Quinolone_Sulfonamides.pdf`) {#ch06-4}

This deck restates quinolone and sulfonamide material already given in §6.1–6.2
above and adds the following.

**Quinolone history and spectrum**: the first quinolone, **nalidixic acid**, was a
byproduct of chloroquine synthesis (an antimalarial drug). Current drugs are
fluorinated 4-quinolones (fluoroquinolones). Spectrum of coverage: broad (both
gram-positive and gram-negative); newer fluoroquinolones also work against
anaerobes.

**Fluoroquinolone generations** (brand names as given):
- 1st gen.: **Nalidixic acid** (Neggram) — moderate gram-negative activity only
  (weak against gram-positive cocci/bacilli, gram-negative cocci); achieves minimal
  serum concentrations; restricted to uncomplicated urinary tract infections; used
  less often today.
- 2nd gen.: **Ciprofloxacin** (Cipro), **Norfloxacin** (Noroxin), **Ofloxacin**
  (Floxin) — expanded gram-negative activity, plus some activity against
  gram-positive and atypical organisms such as *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* and
  *Chlamydia pneumoniae*.
- 3rd gen.: **Levofloxacin** (Levaquin) — retains expanded gram-negative activity
  and shows improved activity against atypical organisms and specific
  gram-positive bacteria (including *Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydia*).
- 4th gen.: **Moxifloxacin** (Avelox) — improved gram-positive coverage
  (including gram-positive bacilli), maintains gram-negative activity, and gains
  anaerobic coverage.

**Mechanism of action** (as given in this deck): targets are DNA gyrase
(topoisomerase II, for gram-negatives) and topoisomerase IV (for gram-positives).
Inhibition of gyrase prevents negative supercoiling in replicating DNA; inhibition
of topoisomerase IV prevents separation of DNA strands.

**Mechanism of resistance**: (1) mutations in chromosomal genes for gyrase and
topoisomerase IV that result in proteins with less binding potential; (2) active
transport out of the cell via efflux pumps.

**Pharmacokinetics**: well absorbed after oral administration; food does not
impair absorption but may delay time to peak serum concentration. Bioavailability
of fluoroquinolones is more than 50% for all agents (up to 95% for some).
Bactericidal; killing is concentration dependent (AUC/MIC). Excretion: most are
cleared by the kidney (adjust dosage for renal patients); exceptions are
**pefloxacin and moxifloxacin**, which are metabolized by the liver — do not use in
hepatic failure.

**Pharmacology of select quinolones** (restating and lightly extending §6.2):
ciprofloxacin (2nd gen.) is particularly useful for infections caused by many
*Enterobacteriaceae* and other gram-negative bacilli (e.g. traveler's diarrhea), is
the most potent fluoroquinolone for *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, is used as an
alternative to more toxic drugs such as aminoglycosides, may act synergistically
with beta-lactams, and is of benefit in resistant tuberculosis — but is not
effective against serious infections caused by MRSA, enterococci, or pneumococci.
Levofloxacin (3rd gen.) is broad spectrum, used for prostatitis due to *E. coli*
and for STDs including gonorrhea, and for skin infections, pneumonia, and *S.
pneumoniae* respiratory infections. Moxifloxacin (4th gen.) has enhanced activity
against gram-positive organisms (e.g. *S. pneumoniae*) and excellent activity
against many anaerobes, but very poor activity against *P. aeruginosa*.

Toxicity & contraindications (this deck's list): nausea, vomiting, abdominal
discomfort (common); diarrhea and antibiotic-associated colitis (uncommon to
rare); CNS side effects — mild headache and dizziness (common to rare);
nephrotoxicity — crystalluria at 3–4x normal doses; phototoxicity (use sunscreen);
**arthropathy in immature animals** (common) — quinolones must usually not be
given to children, unless benefit outweighs risk.

**Sulfonamides — additional mechanistic and classification detail**:
Sulfonamide is a generic term for derivatives of para-aminobenzenesulfonamide,
themselves analogues of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA); sodium salts are water
soluble. Spectrum of coverage: broad (both gram-positive and gram-negative).
Mechanism: competitive inhibitors of dihydropteroate synthase, so bacteria cannot
synthesize their own folic acid; bacteriostatic in most tissues, can be cidal in
urine.

Folate-pathway diagram (this deck): dihydropteroate diphosphate + PABA →
[dihydropteroate synthetase, blocked by **sulfonamides**] → dihydropteroic acid →
dihydrofolic acid → [dihydrofolate reductase, blocked by **trimethoprim**] →
tetrahydrofolic acid.

Pharmacokinetics of sulfonamides: 70–100% of an oral dose is absorbed from the GI
tract (mostly small intestine, also stomach); distributed throughout all tissues
of the body; readily cross the placenta and reach fetal circulation (hence
contraindicated in pregnancy). Excretion: eliminated mostly by the kidneys into
urine, partially unchanged and partially metabolized; some drugs become insoluble
in acid urine and may precipitate.

Classes of sulfonamides (this deck's grouping, by absorption/use rather than by
duration of action as in §6.1):
- **A. Rapidly absorbed and eliminated**: sulfisoxazole, sulfamethoxazole,
  sulfadiazine. Sulfamethoxazole combined with trimethoprim is widely used to
  treat UTIs, respiratory tract infections, and GI infections.
- **B. Poorly absorbed**: sulfasalazine — poorly absorbed in the GI tract; used to
  treat ulcerative colitis and irritable bowel syndrome; gut bacteria break the
  drug down into sulfapyridine and 5-aminosalicylate; toxicity is attributed to the
  sulfapyridine component, therapeutic action to the 5-aminosalicylate component.
- **C. Topical use**: sulfacetamide and silver sulfadiazine.

Toxicity & contraindications: **A. Urinary tract** — sulfamethoxazole and
sulfadiazine can crystallize in acid urine or in dehydrated patients, causing
urinary obstruction. **B. Hematopoietic system** (rare to extremely rare) — acute
hemolytic anemia (associated with G6PD deficiency in RBCs), agranulocytosis,
aplastic anemia. **C. Hypersensitivity reactions** (common to uncommon).

**Co-trimoxazole** (trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole): shows greater antimicrobial
activity than equivalent quantities of either drug alone; the combination was
selected for synergistic activity and because the two drugs have similar
half-lives. A worked figure (Figure 33.13 in this deck) shows bacterial growth
(*E. coli*, arbitrary units, over 10 hours) under no drug, trimethoprim alone,
sulfamethoxazole alone, and both drugs together — trimethoprim alone and
sulfamethoxazole alone each partially suppress growth to a similar degree, while
both drugs together suppress growth far more than either alone, illustrating
synergism. Resistance to the trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole combination is less
frequently encountered than resistance to either drug alone, because it would
require the bacterium to have simultaneous resistance to both drugs.

Additional brand names for folate-pathway drugs (this deck's own list): inhibitors
of folate synthesis — Mafenide (Sulfamylon), Silver sulfadiazine (Silvadene),
Sulfasalazine (Azulfidine), Sulfisoxazole (Gantrisin); inhibitors of folate
reduction — Pyrimethamine (Daraprim), Trimethoprim (Proloprim); combination —
Cotrimoxazole (Bactrim); urinary tract antiseptics — Methenamine (Mandelamine,
Hiprex), Nitrofurantoin (Macrobid).

**Antiprotozoal drugs** — named but not elaborated in this deck, beyond
metronidazole (below):
- Antimalarial drugs: quinine, chloroquine, primaquine, and (unnamed)
  antimetabolites.
- Other drugs: metronidazole, quinacrine (transcribed in source as "Quinicrine"),
  diloxanide, nifurtimox, suramin.

**Metronidazole**:
- Mechanism of action: anaerobic organisms have electron transport proteins that
  participate in electron-removal reactions; metronidazole is an electron acceptor
  that gets activated inside the cell (cytotoxic once activated), and is activated
  only in anaerobes, to metabolites that damage DNA. (Diagram: an electron transfer
  protein at roughly −460 mV competes with metronidazole at roughly −415 mV for
  2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ inside the nucleus/DNA environment, producing reduced
  metronidazole metabolites.)
- Clinical uses: anaerobes and parasites — amebiasis, trichomoniasis, giardiasis;
  anaerobic bacterial infections including *C. difficile* and anaerobic abscesses.
- Adverse reactions: GI distress, seizures, neuropathy; a **disulfiram effect** if
  taken with alcohol (flushing of the face, headache, nausea, vomiting, chest
  pain, weakness, blurred vision, mental confusion, sweating, choking, breathing
  difficulty, and anxiety); metallic taste.
- Distribution: nearly all tissues, including CSF, saliva, bone, abscesses; IV =
  PO (i.e. comparable exposure by either route). Metabolized in the liver;
  inducer: phenobarbital; inhibitor: cimetidine; lower the dose in hepatic
  disease.

## Ch. 7 — Antiviral Drugs {#ch07}

### 7.1 Viruses and antiviral strategy {#ch07-1}

Viruses: obligate intracellular parasites; consist of a core genome in a protein
shell, some surrounded by a lipoprotein envelope; lack a cell wall and cell
membrane; do not carry out metabolic processes; replication depends on host cell
machinery.

Steps of viral replication: (1) adsorption and penetration into cell, (2) uncoating
of viral nucleic acid, (3) synthesis of regulatory proteins, (4) synthesis of RNA or
DNA, (5) synthesis of structural proteins, (6) assembly of viral particles, (7)
release from host cell.

Antiviral agents block viral entry into the cell, or work inside the cell. Most
agents are pyrimidine or purine nucleoside analogs.

### 7.2 Respiratory virus infections {#ch07-2}

Influenza type A & B; Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV). Best treated by
immunization prior to flu season. Other options used if: allergy; variants not
covered by the vaccine; outbreak among unimmunized individuals (e.g. nursing home).

**Zanamivir & Oseltamivir** — inhibit the enzyme **neuraminidase**; effective
against influenza A and B. Best if administered prior to exposure; when given
within 24–48 hours of symptom onset, reduce intensity and duration of symptoms.
- Oseltamivir: oral, a prodrug; GI disturbances, take with food.
- Zanamivir: not active orally; inhaled or intranasal; not recommended in patients
  with asthma or COPD.

**Amantadine & Rimantadine** — effective against influenza A virus, for both
prevention and treatment; useful in high-risk unimmunized patients. Inhibit viral
uncoating by blocking the viral M2 protein, preventing viral release within infected
cells.
- Rimantadine: extensively metabolized by the liver, eliminated by the kidney, low
  CNS penetration.
- Amantadine: renal clearance (unchanged) — dose adjustment needed in renal
  dysfunction; high CNS penetration/CNS side effects.
- Both should be used with caution in pregnancy and nursing mothers.

### 7.3 Antiherpes agents {#ch07-3}

Named drugs: Acyclovir (prototype), Valacyclovir, Famciclovir, Penciclovir,
Trifluridine, Vidarabine. Indications covered by this section: herpes simplex
infections, varicella zoster virus, cytomegalovirus.

**Cytomegalovirus (CMV)** disease manifestations by organ (diagram): brain
(encephalitis), eye (retinitis), lung (pneumonia), stomach and intestines
(gastroenteritis).

**Acyclovir** — a guanosine analog. Mechanism: (1) converted by viral thymidine
kinase to acyclovir monophosphate; (2) converted by host cell kinases to acyclovir
triphosphate (ACV-TP); (3) ACV-TP competitively inhibits and inactivates viral DNA
polymerases and incorporates into the viral DNA chain, causing premature DNA chain
termination.
- Resistance: alteration in viral thymidine kinase; alteration in viral DNA
  polymerase; cross-resistance with valacyclovir, famciclovir, and ganciclovir.
- Clinical uses: oral, IV, and topical formulations; cleared by glomerular
  filtration and tubular secretion. Uses: HSV-1 and HSV-2, VZV. Side effects:
  nausea, diarrhea, headache, tremors, delirium.

**Valacyclovir** — the L-valyl ester of acyclovir; converted to acyclovir when
ingested. MOA same as acyclovir. Uses: recurrent genital herpes, herpes zoster
infections. Side effects: nausea, diarrhea, headache.

**Penciclovir & Famciclovir** — penciclovir available only topically, for HSV-1,
HSV-2, VZV. Famciclovir is a prodrug of penciclovir, orally active. MOA same as
acyclovir. Uses: acute herpes zoster, genital HSV. Side effects: nausea, diarrhea,
headache.

**Anti-cytomegalovirus (CMV) agents**: Ganciclovir, Valganciclovir, Cidofovir,
Foscarnet, Fomivirsen.

**Foscarnet** — an inorganic pyrophosphate (not a purine or pyrimidine analog; no
phosphorylation needed). Inhibits viral DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase, and HIV
reverse transcriptase. Uses: HSV, VZV, CMV, HIV. Requires frequent IV
administration. Renal excretion. Side effects: nephrotoxicity, anemia, nausea,
hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, arrhythmias.

**Ganciclovir** — an acyclic guanosine analog; requires triphosphorylation for
activation (monophosphorylation catalyzed by a phosphotransferase in CMV, and by
thymidine kinase in HSV cells). MOA same as acyclovir. Uses: only approved for CMV
infections. Side effect: myelosuppression. IV administration.

### 7.4 Antiretroviral agents {#ch07-4}

There are five classes of antiretroviral drugs (as enumerated in the source, with
the currently-available-drugs figure supplying the full set):

1. **Nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs)**: Abacavir,
   Didanosine, Emtricitabine, Lamivudine, Stavudine, Tenofovir, Zidovudine (AZT).
   - Notable toxicities (separate slide): Didanosine — pancreatitis; Lamivudine —
     pancreatitis (as stated in the source); Zalcitabine — peripheral neuropathy;
     Stavudine — peripheral neuropathy.
2. **Non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs)**: Delavirdine,
   Efavirenz, Nevirapine, Etravirine.
3. **Protease inhibitors**: Atazanavir, Darunavir, Fosamprenavir, Indinavir,
   Lopinavir, Nelfinavir, Ritonavir, Saquinavir, Tipranavir. (An earlier slide
   also separately lists Indinavir, Ritonavir, Saquinavir, Nelfinavir,
   Amprenavir.)
4. **Fusion inhibitors**: Enfuvirtide, Maraviroc.
5. **Integrase inhibitor**: Raltegravir.

**Highly Active Anti-Retroviral Therapy (HAART)**: combination therapy of more than
one drug of different classes. Lower doses can be used in combination; low rate of
resistance; fewer side effects.

## Ch. 8 — Antifungal Drugs {#ch08}

Fungal infections are, to a large extent, iatrogenic in nature — associated with
broad-spectrum antibiotic use, indwelling catheters, AIDS, cancer, transplant. The
eukaryotic nature of fungi (resembling human cells) makes them difficult to treat.

**Mycoses** by depth of infection: superficial (cutaneous), subcutaneous, systemic.

### 8.1 Drugs for subcutaneous / systemic fungal infections {#ch08-1}

1. **Polyene drugs** — Amphotericin B.
2. **Echinocandins** — caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin.
3. **Azoles** — ketoconazole, fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole,
   posaconazole.
4. **Pyrimidine antimetabolites** — flucytosine.

**Amphotericin B** — obtained from *Streptomyces nodosus*. Mechanism: affinity for
ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane, forming a micropore that disrupts membrane
function and causes cell death (illustrated: polyene + ergosterol → ergosterol with
pore). Fungicidal or fungistatic. Effective against a wide range of fungi: *Candida
albicans*, *Histoplasma capsulatum*, many strains of *Aspergillus*.
- Adverse effects: **acute, infusion-related** — chills, fever, dyspnea, nausea,
  vomiting, bronchospasm, convulsions; thrombophlebitis; hemolytic anemia; renal
  toxicity (decreased renal GFR); hepatic damage; hypotension and hypokalemia
  (interacts with digoxin).

**Azoles** — mainly fungistatic. Triazoles: ketoconazole, fluconazole, itraconazole,
voriconazole, posaconazole. Imidazoles (cutaneous use): clotrimazole, econazole,
miconazole.
- Mechanism (diagram): inhibit the P450-dependent conversion of **lanosterol** to
  **ergosterol** (example: ketoconazole blocking this step) — inhibition of
  ergosterol synthesis disrupts fungal membrane function and increases
  permeability.
- Azoles and amphotericin B should not be used together, because the resulting
  decrease in ergosterol in the fungal membrane reduces the fungicidal action of
  amphotericin B. Azoles are teratogenic.

**Echinocandins: caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin** — interfere with fungal
cell wall synthesis by inhibiting the synthesis of beta (1,3) D-glucan, leading to
cell lysis and death. Specific for *Aspergillus* and *Candida*. Available for
intravenous use only. Rapid infusion causes a histamine-like reaction (flushing).

### 8.2 Drugs for cutaneous mycotic infections {#ch08-2}

Fungi causing superficial skin infections are called dermatophytes or tinea
(ringworm).

1. **Systemic drugs**: Terbinafine, Griseofulvin.
2. **Topical drugs**: a polyene (Nystatin); azoles (miconazole, econazole,
   butoconazole, clotrimazole); Terbinafine.

**Terbinafine (Lamisil®)** — fungicidal; oral and topical administration; highly
protein bound (deposited in skin, nail, adipose tissue). Therapeutic uses:
dermatophyte onychomycosis (nail infection), tinea capitis (oral treatment), tinea
pedis (topical treatment). Contraindicated in nursing mothers.
- MOA: a non-competitive inhibitor of squalene epoxidase in ergosterol synthesis.
- Liver metabolism by CYP450; renal excretion (avoid in renal dysfunction).
- Side effects: GI disturbances; CYP2D6 inhibitor.
- Naftifine and butenafine: topical treatment (related agents).

**Nystatin** — a polyene antifungal drug; binds to ergosterol, forming pores in the
membrane that lead to K⁺ leakage and fungal death. Not absorbed orally; very toxic
if given parenterally; used only for local antifungal effect — oral and vaginal
candidiasis, cutaneous candidiasis.

**Griseofulvin** — binds to microtubules, disrupting mitosis. Deposits in keratin
layers. Used for infections of skin, hair, nails; requires prolonged therapy.
Toxicity: headache, neuro- and hepatotoxicity, photosensitivity, carcinogenic. CYP450
inducer. Contraindicated in pregnancy.

**Other azoles/imidazoles — Clotrimazole, Miconazole**: effective for topical
treatment of tinea infections, oral/vaginal/cutaneous candidiasis, and pityriasis
versicolor.
