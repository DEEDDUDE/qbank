## Lab 1 — Microscopy: Use of the Oil Immersion Objective {#lab01}

> **Source:** Manual, Exercise I, pp. 4–6. Supplemented by `2024-slides/MicroLab-Slides-
> inner/2024 Slides/Lab 2.pdf` pp. 2–5, 14.

Microbiology gives the student an opportunity to become familiar with the use of the
oil-immersion lens — a review of elementary microscope use, especially the oil-immersion
lens, assuming prior use and knowledge of the compound microscope.

**Substage condenser:** a compound lens system below the specimen platform (stage),
generally with an iris diaphragm to regulate the amount of light passing into the
microscope. The condenser permits the microscopist to focus a cone of light exactly on
the object being examined.

**Critical illumination:** achieved by first focusing on the object under examination,
then closing the iris diaphragm until light is almost extinguished, then raising or
lowering the substage condenser until the interior edge of the iris diaphragm's leaves is
sharply seen. Then the iris is opened until the entire microscope field is just exposed
(with a 10X eyepiece and a 97X oil-immersion objective, the diameter of the specimen area
examined — the real field — is about 0.15 mm). This is generally impossible to accomplish
with lower-power objective lenses, presenting a problem at low magnification: inability
to fill the entire field with light regardless of substage condenser position. Some
microscopes solve this by "swinging in" an accessory lens at the bottom of the substage
condenser.

**Inclination joint:** the microscope must never be tilted when wet preparations are
being examined.

**Objective lenses:** mounted on the revolving nosepiece, each color-coded and marked
with numerical aperture (N.A.) and focal length/magnification (X). The oil-immersion
lens is red-banded, 1.25 N.A., 1.9 mm, 97X.

The oil-immersion objective is the most highly magnifying objective on light
microscopes. Its poorer light-gathering ability is evident from its smaller outermost
lens face. The cone of light from the substage condenser should be focused on the
specimen; as light "leaves" the specimen toward the objective it is not collimated and
does not all reach the objective. Even without a specimen, light bends leaving the
microscope slide because the refractive index of glass (1.52) differs from air (1.0).
The resulting angular aperture of light is then larger, and in effect less light reaches
the objective, with a concomitant decrease in resolving power (ability to distinguish
two close objects as separate). Immersion oil placed between specimen and objective
substantially reduces this bending, since its refractive index (1.515) matches glass.
The oil-immersion objective is practically useless unless immersion oil is used. Never
put immersion oil on any objective but the oil-immersion objective.

**Coarse and fine adjustment knobs:** always focus upwards while looking into the
eyepiece. Do not lower the body tube toward the specimen while looking into the
eyepiece unless the specimen is already in "good" focus — then use only the fine
adjustment.

The safest use of the oil-immersion lens requires first focusing the specimen using
lower-power objectives (i.e., high dry), then swinging in the parfocal oil-immersion
lens and focusing by slight adjustment.

After use: swing out the oil-immersion objective and immediately wipe drying immersion
oil from it with lens paper or a special cotton gauze square — if unavailable, leave the
oil on the objective. Never use anything else to wipe any microscope lens. Swing in the
lower-power objective and rack down to the safety stop before storing the microscope.

The microscope diagram in the Manual labels: ocular lens (eyepiece), body tube, arm,
revolving nosepiece, objectives, stage, stage clips, coarse adjustment knob, fine
adjustment knob, diaphragm, light source, base.

### Refractive index and staining prerequisites {#lab01-1}

> **Source:** `2024 Slides/Lab 2.pdf`.

Techniques of light microscopy: 1) reflected light microscopy; 2) transmitted light
microscopy — bright-field, phase contrast, fluorescent, darkfield.

**Refractive index:** air = 1, glass = 1.52, oil = 1.515 — the near-match between glass
and immersion oil is what the Manual's account above explains functionally.

**Stain:** material used to facilitate observation of microorganisms. Dyes are
classified by their chromophore (the part of a molecule responsible for its color) as
acidic (chromophore is a negative ion) or basic (chromophore is a positive ion), at pH 7.
Bacteria carry a net negative charge, so the chromophore of most microbiological staining
dyes is a positive ion (basic). Most commonly used: crystal violet, safranin, methylene
blue, basic fuchsin.

**Staining types:**
1. **Simple staining** — a single stain applied to a bacterial smear (e.g., aqueous
   crystal violet 1%), used to observe morphology and arrangement of organisms.
2. **Complicated (differential) staining** — multiple steps (e.g., Gram stain: four
   steps), differentiating organisms by susceptibility to drugs including penicillin
   (Gram-positive organisms are generally more susceptible to penicillin G).

### Manual's classification — by nutritional completeness {#lab02-1}

Suitable quality culture media for cultivating microorganisms is required for successful
isolation of aetiological agents. Bacteriological media can be broadly sub-divided into
four categories:

1. **Ordinary culture media** — a simple, nutritionally defined medium, e.g., nutrient
   broth, nutrient agar.
2. **Enriched media** — a complex medium that is not completely nutritionally defined.
   Certain organisms do not grow on ordinary nutrient media and require growth-promoting
   ingredients such as blood, glucose, serum, or egg, e.g., blood agar, chocolate agar,
   Loeffler medium.
3. **Enrichment media** — liquid media containing chemical constituents that inhibit
   normal flora and allow pathogens present in small numbers in the specimen to grow,
   thus enriching them. Isolated colonies of these organisms may be obtained by
   subculturing onto solid media. Example: selenite broth, used for primary isolation of
   enteric bacteria from stool specimens.
4. **Differential and selective media** — e.g., MacConkey agar contains crystal violet
   and bile salts, which inhibit Gram-positive bacteria and select for Gram-negative
   rods. MacConkey agar also contains lactose as a substrate and neutral red as an
   indicator — bacteria fermenting lactose produce acid, changing the indicator color so
   colonies turn red; red lactose-fermenting colonies can be differentiated from pale
   non-lactose-fermenting colonies. Selective media selectively permit growth of
   pathogens and inhibit commensals; some also differentiate the pathogen from commensals
   by colony color and opacity, e.g., blood tellurite medium for *Corynebacterium
   diphtheriae*.

**Transport media** are frequently used to sustain organism viability when a clinical
specimen is transported to the laboratory — they prevent contaminant outgrowth during
transit and sustain the pathogen. Stuart media is an example.

Presently, a wide range of culture media are available commercially as dehydrated media,
reconstituted by weighing required quantities and adding distilled water per
manufacturer instructions. The exercise has students prepare 500 mL each of: nutrient
broth, nutrient agar, MacConkey agar, blood agar, trypticase soy agar, and tryptone
cysteine yeast extract agar.

### 2024 deck's classification — by function {#lab02-2}

> **Source:** `2024 Slides/Lab 1.pdf`.

**What is culture media?** Classification of culture media based on function.

- **Basal/ordinary media** — most common in routine diagnostic laboratories (nutrient
  agar). Nutrient broth (NB) consists of peptone, meat extract, water, NaCl. NB + 0.5%
  glucose = glucose broth. NB + 2% agar = nutrient agar. Agar concentration 0.2–0.5% =
  semi-solid medium.
- **Enriched media** — substances like blood, serum, egg added to the basal medium; used
  to grow bacteria exacting in their nutritional needs. E.g., blood agar, chocolate agar.
  Blood agar shows three types of hemolysis: alpha, beta, gamma.
- **Selective media** — the inhibitory substance is added to solid media to increase the
  number of the desired bacterium. E.g., MacConkey's medium, thiosulfate citrate bile
  salts sucrose (TCBS) agar.
- **Differential media** — e.g., MacConkey agar: lactose fermenters give pink colonies;
  non-lactose fermenters give colorless colonies.
- **Indicator media** — e.g., urease medium: urease-producing bacteria produce urease,
  which converts urea to CO2 + NH3; the NH3 turns the medium pink.
- **Transport media** — e.g., buffered glycerol saline.
- **Sugar media** — containing any fermentable substances (shown as a color-change tube
  series).
- **Synthetic media** — prepared from pure chemical substances, exact composition known,
  used for special studies (e.g., metabolic requirement studies). E.g., peptone water.

**Culture methods include:** streak culture, lawn culture, stroke culture, stab culture,
pour plate culture, liquid culture, anaerobic culture.

**Anaerobic culture methods:** production of a vacuum; displacement of oxygen with other
gases; chemical method; biological method; reduction of medium. Example: the candle jar
— inoculated plates are kept with a burning candle, which uses up most of the oxygen
(a little O2 is left, but the presence of CO2 stimulates most bacteria).

**Agar** itself: universally used for preparing solid medium, obtained from the seaweed
*Gelidium*, not affected by bacterial growth, melts at 98°C and sets at 42°C; 2% agar is
employed in solid medium.

**Types of culture media by consistency:** solid (2% agar — colony morphology,
pigmentation, hemolysis can be appreciated), liquid (no agar, for inoculum preparation,
e.g., nutrient broth), semi-solid (0.5% agar, e.g., motility medium).

**Types by ingredients (2023 deck, same categories, different examples/wording):**
simple/basal medium, complex medium (provides special nutrients — added complex
ingredients such as yeast extract, consisting of a mixture of many chemical species),
special medium, synthetic medium.

**MacConkey agar composition (2024 deck):** contains crystal violet and bile salts to
inhibit Gram-positive bacteria; lactose and a pH indicator (neutral red) to determine
lactose-fermenting ability.

### Environmental plate for growth of bacteria {#lab03-1}

Investigates growth of microorganisms from different environmental areas. Unwanted
microorganisms that get onto culture media and grow are termed contaminants. Solid
nutrient medium is used; colonies forming on the plate after incubation usually result
from division of a single cell, representing a pure culture.

Working in groups of four (2 nutrient agar plates, 2 tryptic soy agar/TSA plates, 2
sterile swabs, 1 tube of 5 mL sterile water), each student does one of: a) place fingers
on the nutrient agar plate and rub around; b) swab the tongue then swab a TSA plate
surface; c) swab teeth and gums then swab a TSA plate surface; d) dip a sterile swab in
sterile water, rub off the desk top, then swab the nutrient agar plate surface. Incubate
at 37°C for 18–24 hrs; observe for microbial growth; compare results across groups;
describe colony morphology.

### Effect of disinfectants and antiseptics {#lab03-2}

Working in groups of four, with sterile forceps and alcohol, 3 disinfectants or
antiseptics, 9 sterile filter paper discs, 2 nutrient agar plates, 1 TSA plate, 1 sterile
swab, 1 tube of nutrient broth (for mouth swab), and cultures of *E. coli* in broth, *S.
aureus* in broth, and a swab of mouth flora in broth:

1. Inoculate one nutrient agar plate with *E. coli* by swabbing the surface in three
   directions; dispose of the swab in the disinfectant jar. Using sterile forceps, dip
   sterile filter paper discs in each antiseptic, drain excess fluid, and place the disc
   on the plate surface; mark the plate with the disinfectant used. Incubate at 37°C.
2. Repeat for *S. aureus*.
3. Swab the mouth around the teeth, moisten the swab in a small amount of nutrient
   broth, then swab a TSA plate surface in three directions; dispose of the swab in the
   disinfectant jar. Repeat the disc procedure.
4. Observe plates after 18–24 hrs incubation; measure the diameter of the zone of
   inhibition around the filter paper discs.

**Disinfectants tested (per the "Labs 3 & 4" worksheet, Part 2B):** Detol, 70% alcohol,
95% alcohol — recorded as zone-of-inhibition diameter (mm) for *E. coli* (N.A. medium),
*S. aureus* (N.A. medium), and oral mouth flora (TSA medium).

### Effect of temperature on growth {#lab03-3}

Each microorganism grows best at its optimum temperature — usually the temperature of
its normal habitat. If temperature is lowered, growth is inhibited; if raised well above
the optimum, the organism is killed.

Working in groups of four (8 tubes of nutrient broth; cultures of *Bacillus subtilis*
and *Escherichia coli*):
1. Inoculate 4 tubes of nutrient broth with *B. subtilis*.
2. Inoculate the remaining 4 tubes with *E. coli*.
3. Incubate 1 tube of each culture at 4°C, one at 25°C, one at 37°C, one at 55°C.
4. Observe the tubes after 24–48 hours for growth, and again after 5–7 days. Note growth
   amount per tube as O = no growth, + = little growth, ++ = good growth. Classify each
   culture as mesophile, psychrophile, or thermophile.

**Worksheet objectives (Labs 3 & 4):** 1) investigate the ubiquity of microorganisms;
2) assess the value and benefit of disinfectants in the microbiology lab; 3) study the
effects of temperature on bacterial growth; 4) become familiar with the terms
mesophilic, thermophilic, psychrophilic, optimum temperature, disinfectant, antiseptic;
5) become acquainted with an important microbiology lab technique — aseptic technique.

**Discussion questions (from the Manual):** Did you get growth on all plates — same
morphology, why? Which disinfectant was best, and how did the same disinfectant affect
different bacteria? In theory 70% alcohol is more efficient than 95% alcohol as an
antiseptic/disinfectant — why, and did your result match? What is the difference between
low- and high-temperature effects on bacterial growth? What was the optimal temperature
for each organism? Which bacteria tolerated 55°C — explain.

### Overview of complex, selective, and differential media (streak-plating rationale) {#lab05-1}

The metabolic diversity of microorganisms prevents use of a single simple, universally
acceptable isolation procedure. A tactically manageable scheme for the clinical
laboratory is a necessity. Selective culture techniques rely on the differential
response of pathogens and unwanted organisms to the nutritive and inhibitory components
of the medium, the atmosphere, and the incubation temperature — greatly increasing the
probability of separating and isolating pathogens from unwanted normal flora.

**Nutritionally selective media example:** MacConkey agar is selective for Gram-negative
bacteria — its bile salts and crystal violet inhibit Gram-positive (non-inhibited,
Gram-negative) organisms. MacConkey agar is useful for isolating Gram-negative pathogenic
enteric bacilli able to utilize lactose and not inhibited by its bile salt/crystal violet
levels. It contains lactose as the only carbohydrate, preferentially stimulating growth
of lactose-utilizing organisms, and is thus also a **differential** medium — it
differentiates organisms that can ferment lactose from those that cannot.

Most pathogenic microorganisms are nutritionally fastidious, requiring a variety of
growth factors in relatively complex media containing natural substances (blood, yeast,
serum, milk, carbon dioxide, etc.) — media that unfortunately can also support luxuriant
growth of unwanted microbes present in the same specimen, e.g., blood agar (Mueller
Hinton agar plus 5% sheep red blood cells).

**Thayer-Martin Medium** is another complex medium — chocolate agar (Mueller Hinton agar
plus 5% lysed sheep red blood cells) with antibiotics, used to isolate *Neisseria
meningitidis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. These organisms are nutritionally fastidious;
their dietary requirements are met by the combined use of a polysaccharide (cornstarch),
meat and milk protein, vitamins, hemoglobin, amino acids, NAD, cocarboxylase, and other
components. Incorporating the antibiotics vancomycin, colistin, and nystatin selectively
inhibits unwanted microorganisms, since most *Neisseria* are relatively insensitive to
these antibiotics at the concentrations used.

**Indicator/differential media beyond lactose:** some media contain metabolic indicator
substances that may change the colonial appearance of organisms or their surrounding
agar, making organisms that effect these changes more easily found. **Vogel-Johnson
(V-J) medium** is an example — it contains mannitol, potassium tellurite, a pH
indicator, and relatively high salt concentration. Organisms fermenting mannitol change
the phenol red pH indicator yellow, and if they also reduce colorless tellurite to dark,
insoluble telluride, the colony blackens too. Many pathogenic *Staphylococcus aureus*
strains induce both changes, so colonies of *S. aureus* can be more easily picked out on
V-J medium — though other bacteria can also induce these changes, so the colonial
reactions are only presumptive evidence for *S. aureus*. In practice, V-J medium is
helpful but infrequently used for isolating/identifying *S. aureus*.

**Atmosphere as a selective control** (usually inadvertent): obligate anaerobes such as
*Bacteroides* or *Clostridium* species are markedly inhibited by oxygen; obligate
aerobes such as *Bacillus* and *Mycobacterium* species will not grow without it. The
vast majority of organisms, especially enteric flora, are facultative anaerobes,
growing in the presence or absence of most of the oxygen. A small group —
**microaerophiles** — grow only in the presence of very low pO2; example: *Campylobacter
fetus*, a Gram-negative pathogen. Growth ability at reduced pO2 can be used for
selective advantage: medically important facultatively anaerobic, Gram-positive,
hemolytic streptococci grow at reduced pO2 while generally-contaminating aerobic forms
are repressed — these streptococci, when grown at reduced pO2, often produce larger
colonies than when grown in air (colony size is otherwise minute, ~1 mm), and the
oxygen-labile hemolysin they secrete expresses more prominently, making the
characteristic peripheral clearing zones (hemolysis) more obvious.

**Anaerobiosis methods:** the candle-jar (light a candle in a jar with cultures, seal —
burns until most oxygen is consumed then goes out; trace oxygen left will inhibit
certain obligate anaerobes; the optimal CO2 percentage required for isolating some
bacteria is above 5%, approaching 10%, not always achieved in candle-jars, which may
enrich only to 2–3% CO2); commercial gas-packs (water added to a packet, added to a
sealed jar with cultures, possibly with a one-way valve — the chemical reaction produces
either a gas flushing out oxygen or an alkaline product absorbing oxygen); and
non-toxic reducing compounds incorporated into culture media (thioglycolate into solid
or liquid medium, or 0.1–0.2% agar into liquid media, giving a semi-solid consistency
that reduces oxygen diffusion into the liquid column — fluid thioglycolate broth medium,
chopped meat medium, or iron milk medium are sometimes used for cultivating anaerobes).

**Aerobic organisms** are readily grown on agar or broth surfaces in loosely capped
vessels; increased aeration of broth cultures is obtained by bubbling sterile gas
mixtures through the liquid or by shaking. Tight closure of plastic screw-capped tubes
can inhibit bacterial growth by preventing air entry. **Pellicle** refers to the static
membranous surface film of growth in an aerobic broth culture.

**Temperature as a selective control** (not often practical): organisms growing best at
~45°C are "thermophiles"; those growing best between 25–40°C (most human pathogens) are
"mesophiles"; those growing best under 20°C are "psychrophiles." Generally, 37.5°C is
used for cultivating human bacterial pathogens, 32–37°C for human viruses in cell
cultures, and 18–25°C for the mold form of pathogenic fungi.

### Isolation of "unknowns from simulated clinical specimens" {#lab05-2}

In some bacterial infections the causative organism may be found in pure culture (e.g.,
meninges, bloodstream infections); in others (throat, intestinal tract) the etiologic
agent may be only one of several hundred organism types present. To identify an
organism, it must be obtained in pure culture so its characteristics can be studied.
"Streaking," "streaking for isolation," or the streak plate method is one of the most
frequently used separation methods.

Only one organism is inoculated onto a small area of a solid medium made firm by agar (a
seaweed product melting at 100°C but not solidifying until cooled to 40–45°C). With an
inoculating loop, organisms in the specimen are spread over the agar in a series of
streaks, eventually separating individual cells or clumps from each other. As isolated
cells multiply, they produce visible "colonies" — assumed to be pure cultures of one
organism if the founding organisms were separated well enough that colonies (arising
from a single cell) do not touch. If colonies are too close, they become confluent as
they grow, producing a mixed colony that must be restreaked on another plate to separate
individual organisms.

**Exercise procedure — streaking of plates:**
1. Each student makes at least two different streak plates using their "unknown"
   specimen (containing only one microorganism).
2. Teams of 5–6 members (Team A, B, or C); each team receives 5–6 different unknowns,
   labeled with team letter and specimen type: cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), stool (S),
   throat (T), or wound (W). Ten different bacteria were used across the exercise; each
   student's unknown is different, and results are shared team-wide.
3. Watch the instructor's demonstration before working; practice on extra Petri dishes
   of sterile Trypticase Soy Agar first.
4. Select and sign for an assigned unknown specimen; place in a test tube rack, noting
   the specimen type (CSF, S, T, or W).
5. Select the appropriate media: CSF → one blood (Hb) and one chocolate agar plate;
   stool (S) → one MacConkey (Mac), one Hb, and one trypticase soy agar (TSA) plate;
   throat (T) → two Hb plates, one for aerobic growth and the second in a candle jar;
   wound (W) → one each of Hb and TSA. Do not open these plates until ready to streak.
6. Plating and streaking is done with the Petri dish lid raised just enough to permit
   entry of the specimen and inoculating loop only.
7. **Streaking procedure (Figure 1, four areas):**
   a. Transfer a loopful of specimen with a sterile, cooled inoculating loop to a spot
      marked "1"; spread the loopful evenly over Area I.
   b. With a sterile inoculating loop, "stab" (undercut) a portion of the inoculated
      area so organisms present are buried within the blood agar.
   c. Turn the plate ~90°. Without flaming the loop, streak a small portion of the
      invisible surface deposit across an unused portion (Area II), streaks very close
      together, working toward the center; cover the entire Area II (another 1/4 to 1/3
      of the plate). Flame the loop and let it cool.
   d. Turn the plate another ~90°; make the third set of streaks (Area III) similarly,
      from the edge, smearing a small portion of the invisible deposit from Area II.
      After this, the entire plate should be streaked. Flame the loop; whole process
      takes about two minutes. (A fourth streaking step is shown in Figure 1 as
      optional.) Some bacteriologists flame the loop between areas and "pick up" some
      deposit from an earlier area to transfer directly to the next.
   e. Label the dish (name, date, organism/specimen). Top lids may be accidentally
      switched and should not be the only labeled part.
   f. Place Mac and TSA plates in the 37°C incubator inverted (prevents condensation
      water falling onto the agar surface, which would cause confluent-sheet rather than
      distinct-colony growth; inversion is unnecessary at room temperature). Throat-
      specimen duplicate Hb plates: one incubated at 37°C normally, the other handed to
      the instructor for candle-jar incubation — blood and chocolate agar plates are
      normally incubated in a reduced-pO2 environment like a candle jar.

**Note 1:** the inoculating loop's tip must be flat, closed, and set about 10° from the
shank.

**Note 2:** stab-streaking is not always necessary; done here because the exercise
targets certain facultative anaerobic bacteria whose distinctive cultural properties
(specifically the somewhat O2-labile beta-hemolysin of group A *Streptococcus pyogenes*)
display better when grown in lowered pO2 beneath the agar surface.

**Exercise (follow-up):** if the "unknown" streak plates show well-isolated colonies,
prepare Gram stains from these colonies and correlate gross colony appearance with the
Gram reaction and cell morphology after staining.

### Streaking.pptx supplementary media notes {#lab05-4}

> **Source:** `Previous slides/MicroLab-Streaking.pptx`, Dr. Yousef Mahmoud Habes.

**Mueller-Hinton agar** contains (per liter): beef extract 2.00 g, acid hydrolysate of
casein 17.50 g, starch 1.50 g, agar 17.00 g, distilled water 1000 mL. **Blood agar** =
Mueller-Hinton/nutrient agar basic components plus 5% sheep red blood cells.
**Chocolate agar** = Mueller-Hinton/nutrient agar basic components plus 5% *lysed* sheep
red blood cells. **Thayer-Martin medium** = chocolate agar with antibiotics (vancomycin,
colistin, nystatin) — a selective medium for isolating *Neisseria*.

**Vogel-Johnson (V-J) medium** (differential): contents mannitol, potassium tellurite,
pH indicator (phenol red), high salt concentration. Mannitol fermentation gives yellow
color; reduction of colorless tellurite to dark, insoluble telluride blackens the colony.
Many pathogenic *Staphylococcus aureus* strains can induce both changes.

**Selective media by atmosphere:** 1) obligate anaerobes: *Bacteroides* and *Clostridium*
species; 2) obligate aerobes: *Bacillus* and *Mycobacterium*; 3) facultative anaerobes:
the vast majority of organisms, especially enteric flora; 4) microaerophiles:
*Campylobacter*.

**Producing anaerobic conditions:** 1) candle-jar; 2) gas-packs (a gas-deficient
atmosphere: water added to the packet immediately before use, packet added to a sealed
jar of cultures, possibly with a one-way valve — the chemical reaction produces a gas
flushing out oxygen, or an alkaline product absorbing it); 3) incorporation of non-toxic
reducing compounds into culture media — e.g., thioglycolate broth medium.

**Selective control by incubation temperature:** thermophiles grow best around 45°C;
mesophiles between 25–40°C; psychrophiles under 20°C.

### Colony morphology and purity {#lab06-1}

When mechanically isolated bacteria grow on agar media, they form aggregates (colonies)
after repeated cell division — visible to the naked eye, ranging from a few tenths of a
millimeter to several millimeters or occasionally centimeters in diameter. Visible
colony development is often very rapid; some common bacteria produce visible colonies
within a few hours of growth on blood agar at 37°C.

If a colony arose from one cell, it is safe to assume it is a pure colony (copies of
only the same microorganism). Some colonies may be mixed/impure, arising in at least two
ways: if two different bacteria types "stuck" together after streaking, or if different
adjacent colonies become confluent during growth. It is wise to check colony purity by
Gram-staining a small portion — if pure, only one bacterium type will be seen. When
adjacent colonies are almost touching, do not consider them "pure" even if their extreme
edges appear unique and not intermingled; it is generally unwise to use such colonies
without preparing another streak plate. When apparently identical colonies are confluent,
assume the mixture is not homogeneous — study only colonies whose purity has been
confirmed by Gram stain.

Critical examination of colonial morphology is an overlooked exercise, with many facts
useful in distinguishing, identifying, and determining the number of different organisms
growing on agar media — learned by experience of close scrutiny and practice, one of the
real arts of microbiology. It would be naive to believe a bacterium has an identical or
even similar colonial appearance on different media — the organisms studied,
almost without exception, demonstrate discernible colonial-morphology differences on
all supportive media. Even on the same streak plate, colonial morphology may vary; close
scrutiny may reveal tiny colonies "miniaturized" by poor streaking. Colonies "in a
crowd" are more antagonistic to each other — i.e., smaller than those more distantly
separated — thought to be due to accumulation of metabolic inhibitors rather than a
fall in pH or nutrient depletion.

### The Gram stain — theory {#lab07-1}

In the clinical setting, bacteria are usually observed by light microscopy (transmitted,
phase contrast, fluorescent, and darkfield techniques exist). Stains facilitate
observation; these dyes dissociate into positively and negatively charged ions and are
classified as acidic or basic depending on whether their chromophore is a negative or
positive ion. Bacteria carry a net negative charge at pH 7, so the chromophore of most
microbiological staining dyes is a positive ion — a **basic dye**. Most commonly used:
crystal violet, safranin, methylene blue, basic fuchsin.

If only a single stain is applied to a bacterial smear (e.g., aqueous crystal violet
1%), the method is **simple staining**, used to observe morphology and arrangement.
More complicated, differentiating staining procedures exist; most frequently used is
the **Gram stain**, perfected in 1884 by the Danish physician Christian Gram — a
differential stain.

Depending on technique, there are three or four steps: first the smear is stained with
crystal violet, then Gram's iodine (iodine in KI) is applied, then a decolorizer
(alcohol or acetone-alcohol), then a counterstain (safranin). Bacteria retaining the
crystal violet-iodine complex appear blue-violet-black and are Gram-positive; others
are decolorized and counterstained red with safranin, appearing pink/red — Gram-negative.

From recent evidence, the alcohol or acetone-alcohol mixture alters the wall of
Gram-positive organisms; the altered wall traps the insoluble crystal violet-iodine
complex. These changes do not occur in Gram-negative organisms. Gram-positive organisms
differ from Gram-negative in other respects too: susceptibility to drugs including
penicillin (Gram-positive organisms are generally more susceptible), cell wall
composition, and susceptibility to killing by immune antibodies and to inhibition by
various dyes.

Caution in interpreting Gram stain reactions: Gram-positive organisms are frequently
over-decolorized and appear falsely Gram-negative. False Gram-negative reactions may
also relate to age, growth medium, and pH — as Gram-positive cells age and/or their
growth medium's pH becomes acid, they tend to stain Gram-negative.

**Alcohol's role (per the Streaking/Gram Stain pptx):** alcohol dissolves lipids in the
outer cell membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, allowing the crystal violet-iodine
complex to leak out of the thinner peptidoglycan layer. Recent evidence suggests the
alcohol/acetone-alcohol mixture instead alters the wall of Gram-positive organisms,
trapping the insoluble complex — this alteration does not occur in Gram-negative
organisms. Gram-positive bacteria may falsely appear Gram-negative from: 1)
over-decolorization (use an ethanol-based decolorizer instead of acetone); 2) aging
cells and/or acidic growth-medium pH (add a few drops of aqueous sodium bicarbonate to
correct).

### The 3-Step Gram Stain of Difco {#lab07-2}

A new (1995) procedure developed to permit more control over the decolorization step of
the traditional 4-step technique (Appendix I). Over-decolorization is difficult to
prevent — the solvents used (acetone and alcohol) work relatively fast (1–3 seconds),
and in a warm laboratory the time to over-decolorize is almost equal to the minimum time
needed to perform the step. The 3-Step Gram Stain uses an ethanol-based decolorizer of
unknown proprietary composition; importantly, it lacks acetone (commonly used in the
usual 1:1 alcohol-acetone decolorizer), so besides offering better decolorization
control it is less toxic and considerably less flammable.

**Note:** many other Gram Stain procedure modifications exist. One of the most valuable
is the 4-step procedure, still probably used in most hospital laboratories, described in
Appendix II (not present in this scanned copy — see the front-matter source note).
Another particularly valuable and simple modification adds a few drops of 5% (w/v)
aqueous sodium bicarbonate (household baking soda) to the crystal violet step — the
bicarbonate buffers the stain solution, reducing false Gram-negative reactions that
occur when smears are acidic (acid smears are associated with pus; Gram stains of wound
specimens should be buffered — debris from lysed WBCs is generally acid).

**The 3-Step Bacto-Gram Stain procedure used:**
1. Use air-dried, gently heat-fixed smears.
2. Flood slide with Bacto Gram Crystal Violet for 1 minute; reverse slide, place under
   gently flowing cold tap water to wash off excess dye.
3. Flood slide with Bacto Gram Iodine for 1 minute.
4. Wash off iodine using Bacto 3-Step Gram Safranin-S; flood and stain 10–20 seconds;
   wash off excess dye as in step 2; gently blot dry on bibulous paper pad or air dry;
   examine microscopically using immersion oil and the oil-immersion objective.

Gram-stained bacteria are dead — discard stained smears in the paper trash. Unstained
bacteria are alive and dangerous — discard unstained smears in the autoclave pans.
Assessment of the Gram Stain reaction is based on the staining reaction of the majority
of cells; some bacteriologists consider the reaction at the smear's periphery most
reliable.

### Gram stain exercise procedure {#lab07-3}

1. **Preparation of smears for staining:** share cultures; obtain one prepared 18-hr TSA
   slant each of *Escherichia coli* and *Staphylococcus aureus*. Using a loop, place a
   drop of water on a clean microscope slide; use a sterile inoculating needle to
   transfer a very small amount of bacterial growth to the drop, mixing until a very
   faint, just-visible cloudiness is detected (burn excess growth off the loop/needle,
   avoiding splattering). On a third slide, put a smaller amount of both bacteria in the
   same drop of water. Allow smears to completely air dry. All three smears can go on
   the same slide (mark the underside with three circles); prepare more than one slide
   in case the first Gram-staining attempt is unsatisfactory.
2. Do not heat the slide(s) to dry the suspensions — let them air dry.
3. With the dry smear side up, pass the slide through the burner flame two to three
   times to fix the smear. The slide must be warmed, not scorched, for good fixation;
   heating fixes cells by denaturing proteins and adhering them to the slide. Remember:
   until stained, organisms in dried heat-fixed smears are viable, and therefore
   dangerous.
4. Using air-dried, fixed preparations, Gram stain the microorganisms per the Gram
   stain procedure.

### The acid-fast stain {#lab07-4}

Some bacteria (genus *Mycobacterium*) do not stain by simple staining procedures.
Staining these is facilitated by heat application; even when treated with a
decolorizing agent (acid alcohol), they retain the dye and are designated acid-fast.
This procedure uses initial treatment with carbolfuchsin followed by acid alcohol and
methylene blue. Acid-fast organisms are not decolorized and appear red; non-acid-fast
organisms are decolorized and counterstained blue by the methylene blue.

The causative agent of human tuberculosis, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, is difficult to
stain by most procedures including the Gram stain. Robert Koch, who described the
organism in 1882, developed a successful staining technique to visualize it; his
procedure has been frequently modified, and today the commonly used modification is the
**Ziehl-Neelsen (Z-N) method**. The Z-N method first uses a solution of hot carbol
fuchsin (a mixture of phenol and the red dye fuchsin) to stain the organisms. The
stained smear is then decolorized with a solution of dilute sulfuric or hydrochloric
acid in 95% ethanol ("acid-alcohol"). Methylene blue is often used as a counterstain.
Acid-fast *M. tuberculosis* and other *Mycobacterium* spp. stain red-crimson-magenta;
most other bacteria and cells stain blue.

The acid-fastness of *M. tuberculosis* is associated with the integrity of the cell wall
and its content of rather firmly bound lipids. Phenol apparently aids penetration of the
fuchsin dye. After the carbolfuchsin step, most bacteria will be thoroughly stained; the
unique property of the acid-fast genus *Mycobacterium* is resistance to decolorization
by the acid-alcohol — *Mycobacterium* retains the dye while almost all other
microorganisms are decolorized and subsequently "take" the methylene blue counterstain.

**Microscope demonstration:** examine the acid-fast (AF) stain of *M. tuberculosis* in
sputum from a patient with pulmonary tuberculosis.

**Materials:** organisms — nutrient-agar slant cultures of *Mycobacterium smegmatis*
and *Staphylococcus aureus*; staining solutions — Ziehl's carbol fuchsin, acid alcohol,
methylene blue; strip of blotting paper; egg albumin or serum. Prepared acid-fast
stained smear of tuberculosis sputum.

**Exercise procedure:** smear should be prepared in a small drop of egg albumin or
serum — the protein enhances bacterial adherence to the slide and provides material for
light-background staining.
1. Prepare and fix smears of *M. smegmatis* and *S. aureus*.
2. Cover the smears with a strip of blotting paper.
3. Saturate the paper with Ziehl's carbol fuchsin.
4. Heat the slides to steaming; do not allow them to dry. Continue staining 3–5 minutes,
   then remove the blotting paper.
5. Rinse with tap water; decolorize the smears for 10–30 seconds with acid alcohol.
   Wash slides with tap water.
6. Apply methylene blue for 30–45 seconds; wash, blot dry.
7. Examine the smears under the oil-immersion objective. Acid-fast organisms stain red;
   non-acid-fast organisms stain blue.
8. Record the color of organisms in each preparation and indicate their acid-fast
   reaction.

## Lab 8 — Antibiotic Sensitivity Testing {#lab08}

> **Source:** Manual, Exercise VIII, pp. 37–48. Heavily supplemented by `2024-slides/.../
> 2024 Slides/Lab 3.pdf` pp. 14–34, which supplies worked examples and hand-drawn

### Why antibiotic susceptibility testing matters {#lab08-1}

To effectively manage a patient with infection, a physician must know: 1) the causative
agent; 2) the drugs (antibiotics) that might be used; 3) the patient's history, physical
exam, and laboratory findings. The clinical microbiology laboratory provides information
on the probable etiologic agent, the antibiotics the organism is susceptible to, the
degree of susceptibility, and (if treatment has started) antibiotic levels achieved in
the patient.

After isolating and identifying the probable etiologic agent, the lab will often
routinely test the isolate's antibiotic susceptibilities, even without a specific
request. As a rule, organism identification and sensitivity may be reported as early as
24–36 hours after specimen receipt; slow-growing bacteria may take several days for
complete identification and sensitivities. Organisms often have characteristic
susceptibility patterns to a battery of test antibiotics — usable, with caution, as an
identification aid, just as the Gram stain is used. Sensitivity results may suggest the
most rational therapeutic agent even without knowing the organism's identity — e.g.,
knowing only that an unidentified Gram-positive coccus is resistant to penicillin G may
be an extremely valuable, life-saving finding.

Many microbes quickly become resistant to antibiotics, and the frequency with which
they are encountered may relate to how frequently the antibiotics are prescribed in the
community — bacteria previously understood to be sensitive to an agent may become
resistant. Antibiotic resistance may occur very quickly after therapy begins;
occasionally, after a day or two (sometimes within hours), resistant organisms may be
detected and become part of a patient's dominant flora — continued surveillance is
required.

In practice, the three most commonly encountered antibiotic sensitivity test types are:
1) Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC); 2) Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC),
or Minimum Lethal Concentration (MLC); 3) the Kirby-Bauer or Disc Diffusion test. A
micro-modification of the MIC test exists (MIC by microtest). Two other tests use the
patient's serum: 1) serum assay test; 2) the Antibacterial Activity test (ABA). Finally,
there is the "somewhat revolutionary" E-test.

### 1) Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) {#lab08-2}

The oldest standardized method for determining antibiotic sensitivity — also called the
tube dilution method. The antibiotic is diluted serially in broth across a set of tubes;
the tubes are inoculated with a standardized amount of the isolated organism. After
overnight incubation, the tubes are examined for turbidity with the naked eye —
cloudiness indicates growth, clear tubes indicate no growth. **The MIC is defined as the
minimum antibiotic concentration that will inhibit visible growth.** This test can be
miniaturized. Worked example in the source: a dilution series 12.5, 6.2, 3.1, 1.6, 0.8,
0.4, 0.2, 0 μg/mL, with growth resuming below 3.1 — so MIC = 3.1 μg/mL.

### 2) Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) / Minimum Lethal Concentration (MLC) {#lab08-3}

Although the MIC generally provides the most useful sensitivity information, sometimes
it is desirable to know the antibiotic amount necessary to actually kill the organism
rather than just inhibit growth. Broth from the MIC tubes is incorporated into cooled,
liquefied agar, poured into plates, and allowed to solidify. After 18–24 hours of
incubation, plates are examined; **the lowest antibiotic concentration showing no growth
is the MBC (or MLC).**

MBC's value has been questioned — MBCs are more variable with minor test-condition
changes (less reproducible), vary more between strains of the same species, and are of
less value than MICs in predicting clinical responsiveness. Despite this, antibiotics
are traditionally divided into two categories: **"Bacteriostatic"** antibiotics
generally have MBCs much higher than MICs for most sensitive organisms, and the MBC may
not be surpassable by serum concentrations achievable with clinically useful dosage.
**"Bactericidal"** antibiotics generally have MBCs the same as or only slightly higher
than MICs, and can easily be surpassed by clinically achievable serum concentrations.

**Worked example:**

| | Achievable MIC (μg/mL) | Achievable MBC (μg/mL) | Achievable serum conc. (μg/mL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antibiotic x (bacteriostatic) | 3.1 | 50 | 25 |
| Antibiotic y (bactericidal) | 3.1 | 6.2 | 25 |

Both antibiotics will likely be effective, but many feel "bactericidal" antibiotics are
preferable to "bacteriostatic" ones, especially for infections such as bacterial
endocarditis and for transplant/cancer chemotherapy patients.

Both MIC and MBC help determine the antibiotic levels that must be achieved in body
fluids to affect the probable etiological agent — interpretation must account for
factors not tested in this procedure (protein binding, host defense mechanisms) and the
antibiotic concentration actually achievable in blood, tissues, or urine depending on
administration route. Clinical dosage at 2–3 times the MIC is often used in treatment.

### 3) Kirby-Bauer (Disc Diffusion) {#lab08-4}

Perhaps the most common susceptibility test. A highly standardized form: small paper
discs impregnated with known antibiotic amounts are aseptically placed on agar plates
previously seeded with a known organism concentration. Drugs diffuse from the discs into
the agar at rates characteristic for each agent, producing a concentration gradient; as
the organism lawn grows, zones of inhibition appear around the discs. The zone size
(linear thickness) around a disc is directly related to the log of that antibiotic's MIC
for the tested organism — the smaller the zone, the higher the MIC, and the less
susceptible the organism.

Many factors affect zone size and must be accounted for: "critical zone" sizes are
established per antibiotic, based on the relationship between zone size and MIC, the
antibiotic's diffusibility and solubility, its host toxicity, protein binding *in vivo*,
absorption by tissues, and rate/route of host excretion, etc. An organism with a zone
larger than the adopted critical size is susceptible; a smaller zone is classified
resistant. Zone sizes indicating resistance and sensitivity differ per agent.

Worked example (gentamicin): levels above 12 μg/mL in blood are toxic, so organisms
inhibited only at that range or above are resistant. Organisms sensitive to 3–4 μg/mL
or less are sensitive (2–3 times the MIC is the therapeutic range achievable safely).
Microbes in between are intermediate. Using the regression line for gentamicin, zone
sizes corresponding to these ranges are found; organisms can then be reported resistant,
intermediate, or sensitive based on zone diameter — a zone of inhibition greater than
about 15 mm means the microbe is sensitive to gentamicin.

Certain technical factors also affect zone size: antibiotic concentration in the disc,
agar concentration, medium depth and pH, and incubation atmosphere. The Kirby-Bauer
method successfully minimizes variability in results from day to day and lab to lab
arising from these factors.

**Procedure:** organisms are transferred to a tube of trypticase phosphate, tryptic
soy, or Mueller-Hinton (MH) broth. After brief incubation (2–5 hours), the resultant
broth culture is diluted to a specific turbidity (~10^8 cells/mL). A small aliquot of
this diluted suspension is uniformly spread over 80 mL of Mueller-Hinton agar solidified
in a 150 x 15 mm Petri dish. After inoculation, plates are briefly dried, and no more
than 12 antibiotic discs of prescribed potency are placed on the surface in a standard
pattern. Plates are incubated ~16 hours at 37°C.

After incubation, zones of inhibition are measured with a ruler or a special Single
Disc Zone Chart (Kirby-Bauer). Each chart has specific circle patterns associated with
specific antibiotics — at least one pattern per clinical antibiotic. The Petri dish is
placed over the chart so the disc is centered on its designated pattern (e.g.,
Penicillin G disc uses the P pattern, chloramphenicol the C pattern, streptomycin the S
pattern, etc.). Each pattern's identified center is the exact size of the antibiotic
disc edge; the first ring is the resistant zone boundary (R), the zone between the two
outer rings is the intermediate sensitivity zone (I), and outside the outer ring is the
sensitive zone (S) — if the outer edge of the experimentally determined zone of no
growth falls in the S area of the appropriate pattern, the organism is sensitive to
that antibiotic. R = zone of resistance; I = intermediate zone of resistance; S =
sensitive.

Some antibiotics have two disc patterns to choose from — e.g., penicillin G has P1 and
P2; the choice depends on whether the organism being tested is a staphylococcus (use
P1) or not (use P2), which indicates something about the relative sensitivity of
staphylococci to penicillin G.

Results are reliable only with utmost care in method standardization, including testing
disc potency — otherwise there are too many variables for meaningful results.

| Levofloxacin | 5 μg | ≥22 | 17–21 | ≤23 |
| Gentamicin | 10 μg | ≥14 | 10–13 | ≤15 |
| Azithromycin | 15 μg | ≥16 | 11–15 | ≤17 |

### 4) Serum assay {#lab08-5}

Tests for the amount of antibiotic actually present in the patient's body. Usually
performed on serum, though other body fluids (spinal fluid, urine) can be used. Assays
are generally requested for two reasons: 1) to determine whether a patient is receiving
adequate antibiotic doses to treat their infection; 2) to monitor toxic drug levels.

**Procedure:** an agar plate is seeded with a standard amount of an organism of known
sensitivity to the drug being tested. Paper discs impregnated with known, decreasing
antibiotic amounts are placed on the plate, along with a disc containing a known amount
of the patient's serum. After incubation, zones around each antibiotic disc are
measured, and a curve is drawn relating zone size to known antibiotic amount. The zone
size around the patient's serum-impregnated disc is measured and compared to the curve
to find the corresponding antibiotic concentration.

(2024 deck's worked example shows a 5-disc plate with measured zones of 3–9 mm and a
concentration-vs-zone-size graph used to read off a serum concentration of 5 μg from a
zone size of ~6 mm.)

### 5) Antibacterial Activity (ABA) {#lab08-6}

A combination of the MIC test and the serum assay test. The patient's serum (or other
body fluid) is serially diluted across a series of tubes; each tube is inoculated with a
standard amount of the infecting organism isolated from the patient. Tubes are
incubated 14–24 hours and examined for turbidity.

Dilution of the patient's serum correspondingly dilutes the antibiotic present. **The
highest serum dilution that visually inhibits organism growth is the ABA.** An ABA of
1:4 is desirable, since it indicates the serum contains about four times the amount of
antibiotic needed to inhibit the organism (four times the MIC). Worked example: serum
dilutions 0, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64, 1/128 — determining an ABA of 1:4 in the
figure shown.

### 6) The E-test {#lab08-7}

As resistant microbe strains become more prevalent, therapeutic agent choice narrows;
concomitantly, quantitative information from MICs, MBCs, or other assays not covered in
this exercise (killing-curves, post-antibiotic effects) becomes clinically valuable,
particularly for life-threatening infections such as bacteremias and meningitis, where
pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data are valuable. Although frequently requested and
performed, MICs and MBCs are considered by some to be cumbersome, time-consuming, and
not highly reproducible — hence the development of the E-test.

The E-test (AB Biodisk, Solna, Sweden) uses a non-reactive, impermeable plastic strip
(50 x 5 mm) impregnated on one side with a predefined antibiotic gradient, generally
covering a continuous MIC range corresponding to 15 two-fold dilutions; gradient values
are visibly numbered on the reverse side. Over 100 antimicrobials are available in the
product range, for aerobic bacteria and fastidious organisms (pneumococci, *Haemophilus*,
*H. pylori*, meningococci, gonococci, anaerobes, fungi, mycobacteria).

**In practice:** the test organism, diluted to standard turbidity, is spread on a
Mueller-Hinton agar plate to produce confluent growth. The E-strip is aseptically
applied to the dried, inoculated agar surface and incubated overnight. The antibiotic
diffuses from the strip to produce a continuous agar gradient; after incubation, the MIC
is read from the test strip at the point where the elliptical zone of inhibition
intersects the strip.

"The E-test's role in the clinical assessment of a drug's value is almost unlimited"
(M. L. Sanchez and R. N. Jones, *The Antimicrobic Newsletter* 8:1, 1–8, 1993). At
present, the E-test's major drawback is its high cost.

**Lab 8 worksheet objectives:** 1) introduce the three most commonly used antibiotic
sensitivity tests (MIC, MBC, disc diffusion); 2) become familiar with terms: minimum
inhibitory concentration, minimum bactericidal concentration, bacteriostatic and
bactericidal, resistant, intermediate, susceptible; 3) perform the disc diffusion test
(Kirby-Bauer test); 4) become familiar with serum assay test, antibacterial activity
test, E-test.

**Discussion questions (from the Manual):** What does narrow or broad spectrum
antibiotic mean? Were there colonies found within zones of inhibition — if found,

What is the clinical significance of antibiotic sensitivity testing? How does the K-B

## Lab 9 — Lactobacillus Activity in Saliva {#lab09}

> **Source:** Manual only, Exercise IX, pp. 49–52. No supporting slide deck exists for
> this exercise.

Tooth decay has been linked to normal flora bacteria in the oral cavity. Oral flora
bacteria can form plaque and become embedded in tooth lesions. Through a combination of
acid production by these bacteria and decomposition of tooth structure, dental caries
form and increase in size.

Bacteria growing in saliva and on tooth surfaces ferment available carbohydrates to
produce lactic acid. Most of these bacteria can ferment sucrose (table sugar) and
complete its conversion to lactic acid in plaque deposits on the teeth or in the saliva,
before sucrose-containing food is completely chewed and swallowed (those with inflamed
gums or teeth with dental caries feel pain as the acids produced irritate the lesions).
Lowered pH increases enamel solubility, resulting in dental caries. Plaque formation and
food/bacteria lodging in tooth lesions create anaerobic conditions that enhance acid
production through fermentation. When these organisms produce polymers of dextran, the
lactic acid is held against the tooth surface as plaque, enabling breakdown of tooth
enamel and dentin — resulting in more tooth decay. Sucrose is also important in forming
the dextran layer.

**Method:** Rogosa SL agar — a low-pH, high-acetate medium that suppresses most oral
flora except the lactobacilli. (The Manual poses this as a question to the student: "Is
this medium selective or differential?")

In the Rogosa test, direct pour plate counts of bacteria in saliva are made using
Rogosa SL agar. This medium is highly selective for the genera *Streptococcus* (S) and
*Lactobacillus* (L); the number of colonies developing is assumed representative of
bacterial activity in the oral cavity. The actual number of acid-producing bacteria in
saliva is determined this way; the procedure assumes some direct relationship between
numbers measured under laboratory conditions and the actual acid production on tooth
surfaces.

**Susceptibility-to-dental-caries interpretation table (Rogosa Test):**

| Susceptibility to dental caries | Approximate number/mL |
|---|---|
| Low | <100/mL |
| High | <10,000/mL |
| Very high | >10,000/mL |

**Source note:** the table as printed states "High" susceptibility corresponds to
"<10,000/mL" — the same *less-than* direction as "Low," which reads as an inconsistency
against "Very high" being "*greater* than 10,000/mL" (one would expect "High" to be a
range, e.g., 100–10,000/mL, not repeat "<10,000"). Preserved exactly as printed rather
than corrected, since the intended range cannot be reconstructed without guessing.

The test's value lies in the relationship between bacterial acid production and dental
caries formation. Its accuracy and reliability depend on correct sampling, accurate
test reading, and understanding the procedure's limitations. Important variables:
timing of the sampling period (before/after brushing and flossing), early or late in
the day (food accumulation), and diet nature (high in sucrose). Variation among
individuals, especially in dental caries susceptibility, also influences results.

**Laboratory objectives:** this exercise uses the Rogosa test to determine individual
susceptibility to dental caries. By completion, students will: understand the
relationship between fermentation and acid production in saliva and on teeth;
understand the procedure used to estimate bacterial count; understand the relationship
between dietary sugar and streptococcal/lactobacillus activity in the oral cavity.

**Materials needed:** three tubes of SL Rogosa agar (9 mL) per saliva sample tested;
sterile test tube; sterile pipettes; 3 sterile Petri plates.

**Laboratory procedure:**
1. Obtain 3 tubes of Rogosa test agar; melt the tube and hold at 50°C.
2. Chew the paraffin block to stimulate saliva production; collect at least 2.0 mL of
   saliva in a sterile test tube.
3. Transfer 1 mL of saliva into the first tube of Rogosa test agar; mix thoroughly.
4. Transfer 1 mL from the 1st Rogosa tube into the second; mix thoroughly.
5. Transfer 1 mL from the 2nd Rogosa tube into the 3rd; mix thoroughly.
6. Pour the tubes into sterile Petri plates; let the agar solidify.
7. Incubate at 37°C.
8. Record results on the laboratory worksheet.

## Lab 10 — Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) {#lab10}

> **Source:** Manual, Exercise X, pp. 53–55. Supplemented by `2023-slides/.../2023
> Slides/lab 5.pdf` pp. 15–22, which supplies the ELISA-type diagrams (direct, indirect,
> sandwich, competitive) the Manual does not itself illustrate.

ELISA stands for Enzyme Linked Immuno Sorbent Assay, one of the most widely used assays
in the clinical laboratory. It can detect either antibody or antigen to the picogram
(pg) range. It has largely replaced the radioimmunoassay procedure, since it does not
require dangerous radioisotopes and is easier to perform. It is one of the standard
procedures used for detecting antibodies to the HIV virus.

The ELISA procedure exploits the fact that most proteins bind firmly to the surface of
several kinds of plastic, usually via hydrophobic interactions between the protein's
non-polar residues and the plastic's naturally hydrophobic components. Polystyrene or
polyvinyl chloride are the plastics usually employed — the most common materials in
manufacturing most microtiter plates.

**The test is performed in four steps:**
1. Adding the primary reagent (usually antigen or antibody) to the microtiter plate
   wells, followed by incubation and careful washing of the wells.
2. Adding the secondary reagent (usually an antibody specific for the primary reagent)
   to the same wells, followed by incubation and more washing.
3. Adding the tertiary reagent (an antibody specific for the secondary reagent, which
   has been conjugated by covalent bonding to an enzyme).
4. Finally, adding a reagent to the wells that can be converted by the enzyme into a
   colored compound.

Wells containing the sought substance assume a color directly proportional to the number
of antibody molecules bound to the antigen (primary reagent). The ELISA procedure can
thus be made quantitative or qualitative, depending on the setup method used.

**Worked example in the Manual:** ELISA test for human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) in
serum or urine, using the "Beta-U-Test" — a rapid, sensitive qualitative test for hCG in
urine or serum. This test is a two-sided sandwich ELISA using specific selected
antibodies against hCG.

**Method:**
1. Place one drop (50 μL) of the control, references, and patient samples into
   correspondingly numbered wells.
2. Place one drop (50 μL) of antibody-enzyme conjugate into each well.
3. Mix gently; keep at room temperature for 5 minutes.
4. Wash the wells 5 times with tap water.
5. Add one drop (50 μL) of Chromogen Reagent (containing tetramethyl benzidine in
   methanol) and substrate reagent (containing hydrogen peroxide in buffered solution)
   to each well.
6. Mix gently; keep at room temperature for 5–10 minutes.
7. Observe any color change and compare sample wells with positive and negative
   references and control wells.

### Types of ELISA {#lab10-1}

> **Source:** `2023-slides/.../2023 Slides/lab 5.pdf` pp. 15–22, "Exercise 10 — ELISA."
> Genuinely new content beyond the Manual — the Manual describes only the sandwich-ELISA
> hCG worked example above; this deck illustrates all four ELISA formats.

ELISA is described as a widely used biochemical technique for detecting the presence of
specific proteins, antibodies, antigens, or other molecules in a sample, via
**immunocomplex** formation. Four types:

- **Direct ELISA:** 1) antigen is coated onto wells by passive adsorption and
  incubation; 2) an antibody conjugated with an enzyme (labeled antibody) is added and
  incubated with the antigen; 3) substrate/chromogen is added and color develops
  (signal).
- **Indirect ELISA:** 1) antigen is coated onto wells by passive adsorption and
  incubation; 2) a primary antibody is added and incubated; 3) an anti-species antibody
  conjugated with enzyme (labeled antibody) is added and incubated with the antigen;
  4) substrate/chromogen is added and color develops (signal).
- **Sandwich ELISA:** 1) a capture antibody and blocking buffer are set in the well;
  2) antigen is added; 3) a biotin-labeled detection antibody is added; 4)
  streptavidin-HRPO is added; 5) TMB substrate is added, producing a colored product.
- **Competitive ELISA:** antibody is incubated with the antigen to be measured; the
  Ag-Ab mixture is added to an antigen-coated well; wash; enzyme-conjugated secondary
  antibody is added; wash; substrate is added and color is measured.
